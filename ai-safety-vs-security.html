AI SafetyversusAI Security

Safety guards against harm nobody meant to cause; security guards against harm someone does mean to cause. Trustworthy AI needs both.

An illustrated guide to “AI Safety vs. AI Security: Demystifying the Distinction and Boundaries” by Zhiqiang Lin, Huan Sun and Ness Shroff, The Ohio State University (arXiv 2506.18932, June 2025).

People often say “AI safety” and “AI security” as if they meant the same thing. They don’t. When an AI system causes harm, it usually happens in one of two ways: the system gets something wrong on its own, or a person deliberately pushes it into doing something bad. This paper argues that we should keep those two cases apart, because each one needs its own tools, its own experts and its own rules. It also shows that in real systems, one kind of failure very often leads to the other.

1The one question that sorts them

Suppose an AI system has done something harmful. Before asking how to fix it, the paper suggests asking a simpler question first: did anyone intend this?

If nobody meant for it to happen, the problem belongs to AI safety. The cause might be a software bug, a skewed training set, a situation the system never saw during training, or a goal that was specified slightly wrong. The “opponent” here isn’t a person at all. It’s complexity, uncertainty and our incomplete understanding of how the system behaves.

If someone did mean for it to happen, the problem belongs to AI security. Now there is a thinking opponent who studies the system, looks for weak spots and adapts when you defend against them.

2How the words got tangled

If the distinction is this simple, why do people mix the terms up? Mostly because of history. The small diagrams below show how much ground the word “safety” was asked to cover at each point.

  1. Mid-2000s onwardWorries about future superintelligence“AI safety” began as a field about keeping hypothetical, very powerful future AI beneficial and controllable. The concern was the AI’s own behavior, not human hackers.
  2. 2016“Concrete Problems in AI Safety”Amodei and colleagues framed safety as preventing accidents in machine learning systems, and treated security as a separate, closely related field.
  3. 2024Qi et al.: risk management needs bothA group of researchers noted that the two communities grew up apart and define the terms inconsistently. They pointed to aviation and nuclear power, where accidents and attacks are handled as different problems.
  4. January 2025International AI Safety ReportThis major report used “safety” as an umbrella that also covers malicious use, such as AI-powered cyberattacks and disinformation.
  5. June 2025This paperDraws a clean line by intent. Unintended harm is safety. Deliberate attack is security. The two are studied separately, then managed together.

A broad umbrella term is handy for public debate. But as AI moves into hospitals, cars and power grids, the authors argue that vagueness becomes costly: research gets diluted, funding gets misdirected, and regulations aim at the wrong kind of risk.

3A message on a noisy wire

The paper’s sharpest illustration comes from computer networking. Alice sends Bob a short message. Two very different things can go wrong on the way, and they need two very different fixes.

The first danger is random noise: a flipped bit on the wire. To catch it, Alice attaches a checksum, a short fingerprint computed from the message. Bob recomputes the fingerprint; if it doesn’t match, something got garbled.

The second danger is Eve, an eavesdropper who wants to change the message. A checksum is useless against her, because anyone can compute one. She simply rewrites the message and attaches a fresh, matching checksum. To stop Eve, Alice needs a message authentication code (MAC), a fingerprint that also mixes in a secret key only Alice and Bob share.

Protect the message with
Alice sentMEET AT NOON— Bob received—— Bob recomputes——
Pick a protection method, then choose how the message travels.

The fingerprints are real CRC-32 values. The “MAC” here is a toy (a CRC over the key and message) meant only to show the idea; real systems use constructions such as HMAC.

Try all six combinations. The checksum handles noise perfectly well and fails completely against Eve. That is the paper’s central point in miniature: safety tools are built for a world that is messy but not hostile, while security tools assume an opponent who is watching, thinking and adapting.

Pause and ponder: a stronger checksum would catch rarer noise patterns. Would it help at all against Eve? Why not?

4Two careful definitions

With the intuition in place, here are the paper’s definitions in plain words.

AI Safety

An AI system’s ability to avoid causing harm that nobody intended, to people, environments or institutions, even when its inputs, goals, training data or deployment conditions are uncertain.

Its question: does the system behave as it ought to, even in messy, unpredictable, but non-hostile conditions?

AI Security

An AI system’s ability to withstand deliberate attacks on its data, algorithms or operations, keeping them confidential, intact and available while adversaries are present.

Its question: can an attacker manipulate the system, steal it or poison it?

What safety tries to prevent, and what it aims for

The paper groups safety’s concerns into four kinds of internal problem, each paired with a goal.

What security needs to reason about

Every security problem, the paper says, involves three ingredients at once.

Security’s goal is to protect the three classic properties of any valuable system:

Confidentiality nobody reads what they shouldn’t Integrity nobody changes what they shouldn’t Availability it keeps working when needed

And the attacks against AI in particular come in two families:

Attacks on the data

Poisoning slips bad examples into training data. Model inversion reconstructs private training inputs. Membership inference reveals whether a particular person’s record was used in training.

Attacks on the model

Evasion crafts inputs that trick the model at the moment of use. Model extraction copies a proprietary model by querying it. For chatbots, prompt injection smuggles hostile instructions into the text the model reads.

5Looking inward, looking outward

A helpful way to picture the split: safety is about what can go wrong inside the system, and security is about what can be done to it from outside.

This split also clarifies a confusing word: misuse. If a chatbot gives dangerous medical advice because of gaps in its training data, and someone trusts it, that is a safety failure, since nobody set out to cause harm. If someone crafts a clever prompt to trick the same chatbot past its filters so it writes malicious code, that is a security failure, because the exploitation was deliberate.

6How each feeds the other

Separate definitions do not mean separate problems. The paper stresses that in practice the two are tightly linked, and the links run in both directions.

Security failures cause safety failures. A hacker who takes over a car’s controls (a security breach) can cause a crash (a safety failure). A prompt injection that slips past a chatbot’s filters (a breach) can produce harmful instructions that reach a real user (a safety failure). Safety mechanisms are themselves targets, so good security is often a precondition for safety.

Safety flaws become security openings. A model with predictable biases hands propagandists a lever to pull. A model trained to be overly trusting and helpful is easier to talk into things it shouldn’t do. Unintended weaknesses become intended attack surfaces.

The adversarial cycle

These links can chain into a loop that never quite ends.

Tensions and synergies

Improving one side can quietly cost the other. It can also help both.

More transparency and interpretability
  • Safety gains: easier to debug and to trust.
  • Security may lose: it can reveal weak spots to attackers.
Aggressive adversarial training
  • Security gains: harder to fool with crafted inputs.
  • Safety may lose: accuracy or fairness on ordinary inputs can drop.
General robustness
  • Safety gains and
  • security gains: a model that is steadier in general is harder to break by accident or on purpose.

7A house with two jobs

The paper’s second analogy is a building. An architect makes sure the building stands up, doesn’t burn and lets everyone in safely. A security team makes sure the wrong people can’t get in. Both are necessary, and they are different jobs done by different people.

The paper draws out three lessons from this picture:

Transparency helps both. Inspection panels let people check a building’s structure. Likewise, an interpretable model is easier to debug (safety) and also makes tampering easier to spot (security).

Fail-safes need protection. A sprinkler system is a safety feature, but if a vandal can switch it off, it becomes a single point of failure. An AI emergency shutdown switch likewise needs its own security.

Neither can make up for the other. That is the paper’s fundamental principle, and the four small houses below show why.

8Two different toolboxes

Just as a checksum and a MAC are different tools, the two fields reach for different mechanisms. The paper pairs each one with a part of the building.

Safety: build it sound

Alignment with human values
Training with human feedback (RLHF), Constitutional AI, content filters. Designing a hospital around patients, not looks.
Robustness to natural change
Operating limits and confidence thresholds for unfamiliar inputs. Standing firm through storms.
Interpretability
Explanation methods such as LIME and SHAP that show why a model decided something. Blueprints for maintenance.
Fairness and ethics
Finding and reducing biases that treat groups unfairly. Ramps and elevators for access.
Fail-safes and control
Emergency shutdown and systems that can be safely interrupted. Sprinklers.
Long-term risk
Studying power-seeking and catastrophic risks from future systems. Earthquake-proofing a skyscraper.

Security: keep attackers out

Adversarial robustness
Input sanitization, adversarial training and “circuit breakers” against evasion and prompt injection. Tamper-proof locks.
Data integrity
Validating and encrypting data so training sets can’t be poisoned. Checking the building materials.
Model protection
Watermarking to detect and deter model theft. Guarding the architect’s patents.
Access control and red teaming
Strict permissions following standards such as NIST’s, plus hired attackers who probe for gaps. Security badges.
Resilience
Keeping the system running while under attack. A backup generator.
Secure supply chains
Protecting every step of the pipeline that builds and ships the model. Vetting the contractors.

9Try it yourself

The paper walks through three domains. Pick one to see how each side frames the risks.

Safety

Accurate, reliable diagnoses; handling rare cases; avoiding biased results. Example: an X-ray model misreads scans that contain unusual image artifacts.

Security

Keeping patient data private; protecting models and medical devices from tampering. Example: an attacker poisons training data to skew diagnoses.

Safety

Seeing clearly in edge cases, failing safely, and handling unavoidable-collision dilemmas. Example: perception degrades in a sudden downpour.

Security

Resisting attacks on vehicle controls, sensor spoofing and jammed vehicle-to-vehicle links. Example: hackers spoof GPS or disable the brakes remotely.

Safety

Reducing hallucinations, misinformation, toxic or biased output, and harmful instructions caused by imperfect alignment.

Security

Defending against prompt injection and jailbreaks, keeping data confidential, and preventing deliberate use for disinformation or malware.

Sort these scenarios

For each case, decide which field it belongs to. Remember the one question: did anyone intend it?

    10Bringing them together

    Having pulled the two ideas apart, the paper ends by insisting they be managed as one. Two streams of expertise, each with its own methods, feed a single goal.

    The authors name four supports for that merged approach:

    Shared frameworks and standards

    Risk frameworks that explicitly cover both views, such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and guidance from national AI safety institutes.

    Design both in from the start

    Red teaming that hunts for safety failures and security holes together, and fail-safes built to resist tampering.

    Joined-up safeguards

    Technical checks plus governance, audits and one incident-response process; monitoring that watches for both anomalies and intrusions.

    Mixed teams

    AI developers, safety engineers, security experts, ethicists, lawyers and social scientists working on the same problems.

    Safe by design.Secure by default.

    Safety makes sure an AI system does no harm on its own. Security makes sure nobody can turn it toward harm. Keeping the two ideas distinct lets researchers pick the right tools and lets regulators write the right rules. Keeping them connected is how we end up with systems people can actually trust.
