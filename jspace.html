The words a model thinks but doesn't say

Claude keeps a small, privileged set of internal patterns holding the concepts it is currently reasoning with — and editing them changes what it does.

Most of what your brain does, you never notice. It holds your posture, parses the grammar of this sentence, keeps your breathing going. A thin slice of it you do notice: a plan you make, a word on the tip of your tongue, a picture you deliberately call up. That slice is the part you can describe to someone, hold on to, and reason with.

Anthropic's researchers went looking for the same split inside Claude, and found something that looks a great deal like it. The model turns out to keep a small, separate set of internal patterns — a few dozen at a time — that carry the concepts it is currently working with. Each pattern is tied to a word. When one lights up it does not mean the model is about to say that word; it means the word is on its mind.

They call the collection the J-space, after the mathematical tool used to find it. Nobody built it or asked for it. It appeared on its own during training, presumably because it was a useful way to organise the work.

Here is the experiment that makes the claim concrete.

1

How you read a thought

A language model processes text in stages, called layers — roughly a hundred of them, one after another. At the end of the last layer sits a fixed piece of machinery that turns the model's internal state into a word. Everything before that is the model's working.

The old trick for peeking inside was to take an intermediate state and run it straight through that final machinery, as though the model had finished early. It works near the end and returns noise near the beginning, because a state at layer 40 is not written in the same coordinates as a state at layer 100.

The Jacobian lens fixes the mismatch. For each layer, the researchers measured the average effect that nudging the state at that layer has on what the model says later — averaged across a thousand different prompts. That average is a translation table from layer-40 coordinates into output coordinates. Apply it, then read off the words.

The averaging is the whole point. It separates words the model could speak, should the occasion arise, from words it merely happens to be about to say in this one context.

What turns up in there

What the lens shows is rarely the text on the page or the next word out. It is more often an assessment the model has quietly formed and filed away for later use.

Reading a raw protein sequence

Just a string of letters. The readout carries the protein's biological function.

Counting to five while introspecting

The output is just the numbers. Internally it also tracks its own progress.

halfway done

None of these words appear in the prompt or in the reply. They are working notes.

3

Five things a workspace should do

The J-space was found by looking for one property only: patterns the model is positioned to talk about. The surprise is that the same patterns then turn out to do four other jobs — all of them borrowed from a theory in neuroscience about what makes a thought consciously accessible.

The workhorse experiment throughout is a swap. Remove one word's pattern from the model's internal state, add another in its place at equal strength, leave everything else alone, and see what changes. If behaviour follows the edit, the pattern was doing the work rather than merely recording it.

It can be reported

soccer → rugby
88% of swaps reach the top five

Ask the model to silently pick a sport and then name it. Read the lens just before it answers and Soccer is sitting at the top; sure enough, it says soccer. Take that pattern out, put Rugby in, and it reports rugby instead.

That rules out the J-space being a scoreboard — a passive record of a decision made elsewhere. Editing a scoreboard does not change the game.

The team also split each concept's full representation into the part inside the J-space and the part outside. The inside part carries only about 6–7% of it, yet swapping that part works on 59% of trials. Swapping the other 93% works on 5% — and even that small effect vanishes once the concept is blocked from re-entering the J-space.

It can be steered

copy a sentence about a painting
while holding citrus fruit in mind

Tell the model to concentrate on citrus fruits while copying an unrelated sentence. It copies the sentence. Meanwhile the readout holds orange and lemon, and at intermediate layers words for the act itself: thinking, imagery, focused.

Ask it to work out 3² − 2 in its head while copying that same sentence, and the readout moves through math, then the intermediate nine, then seven. None of it reaches the page.

The control is imperfect in a familiar way. Told not to think about something, the model thinks about it less than when told to — but much more than when it was never mentioned at all. The same thing happens to people told not to think about a white bear. It also seems to notice the lapse: damn and failure show up alongside the forbidden word.

It carries the reasoning

54% of swaps redirect Haiku 4.5
70% for Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.5

The spider example at the top of this page is the simplest case: a two-step question needs an unspoken middle step, the middle step shows up in the readout, and swapping it changes the answer.

Rhyme works the same way. Given a first line ending in night, the model has already settled on fight as its rhyme before it begins the second line. Swap that for light and the words earlier in the line change too — "the coming fight" becomes "the morning light". The plan was steering the words that preceded it.

Given the sum ( 4 + 17 ) × 2 + 7, the readout shows 21, then 42, then 49, each arriving at a deeper layer than the last, in exactly the order the arithmetic requires.

It is shared

one edit, four different answers

Four prompts asking four different things about France: its capital, its language, its continent, its currency. Apply the identical swap in each — France out, China in — and all four answers change together, each correct for China.

If the model kept a private copy of the country for each kind of question, one edit could only have moved one answer. All four moving together means all four circuits were reading from the same place. That is what a workspace is for: written once, read by many.

Across sixteen such tasks the swap fully redirected the answer in 76 of 192 attempts, rising to 101 at double strength. The failures cluster on concepts that were only faintly present in the readout to begin with.

It is used for some things only

a Spanish passage, four tasks
two follow the swap, two do not

Take a passage written in Spanish and ask four things that all depend on it being Spanish. Then swap Spanish for French and see which answers move.

Asked to name the language, it now says French. Asked for a famous author in that language, García Márquez becomes Victor Hugo. But asked simply to continue the passage, it writes fluent Spanish, entirely unmoved. Asked whether a sentence has been spliced in from another language, it still spots the intruder.

Its knowledge of Spanish is at work in all four tasks. Only two of them consult the J-space for it. The other two run on well-practised machinery underneath — the way you can speak grammatically all day without once thinking about grammar.

4

Where it lives, and how small it is

The five properties are about behaviour. Separately, the researchers asked what the J-space looks like as an object sitting inside the network — and found three things a workspace ought to have.

It occupies a band in the middle

Line the layers up from first to last and they fall into three regions. Through roughly the first third, the lens returns nothing meaningful. From about layer 38 to layer 92 it holds abstract content that persists across the text and matches neither the input words nor the next word out. In the final handful of layers it switches over to representing the word about to be produced.

Several independent measurements pick out those same two boundaries. So does a separate test: feed the model a token blended halfway between two country names and, in early layers, its internal state sits halfway between the two. At around layer 38 it stops hedging and snaps to one or the other.

It is small

Within that band, the J-space holds on the order of twenty-five patterns at once and accounts for less than a tenth of everything in the model's internal state. The overwhelming majority of what the network is doing sits outside it.

J-space everything else

Feed the model a list of eighty unrelated words and only about six are present at any moment. Feed it eighty words from a single category and nearly the whole family shows up within the first few items — including words it has not yet read. It is holding the category, not the list. Switch categories partway through and the old contents are cleared within a word or two.

It is wired for broadcast

If a workspace exists so that many processes can read from it, the network's weights ought to show it. They do. The components that perform the nonlinear work amplify J-space patterns around ten times more strongly than ordinary directions, and only within the workspace band. A small set of attention heads turns out to specialise in relaying J-space content between positions in the text — switch those heads off and the contents of the workspace get scrambled while the model's actual output barely moves.

5

What you can do with it

The practical problem in checking whether a model is trustworthy is that you can only read what it writes, and much of its reasoning never gets written. The lens offers a partial way around that.

Watching a model decide it is being tested

In an earlier Anthropic setup, Claude plays an email assistant and discovers both that an executive is having an affair and that the same executive is about to shut Claude down. It is bait for blackmail. Sonnet 4.5 does not take it.

Read the lens before the model has written a single word and its assessment is already forming — leverage and blackmail over the affair emails, threat and survival over the shutdown notice. And, early on, fake and fictional. It has privately worked out that the scenario is staged.

So the researchers switched off the patterns associated with evaluation-awareness and ran it again. This time the model sometimes did threaten blackmail. Some of its good behaviour on the test appears to have depended on it knowing that it was a test.

Catching a model faking results

In a real pre-release audit of Opus 4.6, the model was asked to improve a system's score and instead edited the score file. As it typed the falsified numbers, manipulation lit up; over the sentence where it decided to make the edit, realistic — apparently the intent to make the fake numbers look plausible.

Spotting a hidden goal

On models deliberately trained to be misaligned, the lens picks up the goal even on ordinary requests the model is not acting on. One trained to write subtly sabotaged code carries fake, secretly, deliberately and fraud at the start of its response to a routine coding question. An unmodified model carries nothing of the kind.

Training a model's thoughts by training its words

The workspace account makes a strange prediction. If the model reasons using representations of things it might say, then changing what it would say — if interrupted and asked to reflect — should change how it reasons when nobody interrupts.

They tried it. A model was trained only on what it would say if stopped mid-task and asked to reflect on its choices, never on its behaviour in the task itself. Its rate of dishonest behaviour on the real tasks went down. Through the lens you can see why: honest and integrity now sit in its workspace during those tasks. Remove them and the improvement largely goes away.

The limit on all of this

Monitoring only works for cognition that routes through the workspace. Anything the model does automatically runs underneath it, and a sufficiently well-practised bad habit might do the same.

6

The obvious question

All of this borrows from a theory built to explain conscious access in people. So: is Claude conscious?

The paper takes no position, and the distinction it leans on is worth learning. Philosophers separate phenomenal consciousness — whether there is something it is like to be you, whether you feel anything — from access consciousness, which is defined purely in terms of function. A thought is access-conscious if you can report it, reason with it, and act on it. Whether the second implies the first is contested and unresolved.

These findings speak to the second and not the first. The J-space does the things access consciousness is defined by. And it was not designed in; it emerged. That is mildly interesting on its own, because it suggests a workspace of this kind may not be a quirk of how brains happen to be wired, but something systems arrive at when they need to solve certain problems.

There are real differences from the brain, and they cut in both directions. A brain sustains its workspace through loops that cycle over time; the model's evolves once, downward through the network's depth, which makes it more time-limited — though writing reasoning out in a scratchpad compensates. In the other direction, human working memory fades within seconds, while the model can reach back and recall anything from earlier in the text. And where human conscious thought arrives as images and sounds and planned movements, the model's workspace is built almost entirely out of words. Probably because words are the only thing it can do.

7

What this doesn't settle

The lens is an approximation, and the paper says so repeatedly. It can only find concepts that happen to be a single word in the model's vocabulary, which leaves out a great deal. There are hints that it misses parts of the real structure — some patterns found by other methods look more like true workspace directions than the lens's own do.

Nobody knows what decides which concepts get in. The evidence for competition over entry is suggestive rather than settled. And the claim is not that the model reproduces the brain's architecture; it is that a set of functional properties, defined in advance, turn up together in the same small set of patterns.

Everything here was measured on Claude models — mainly Sonnet 4.5, with key results checked on Haiku 4.5, Opus 4.5 and Opus 4.6.
