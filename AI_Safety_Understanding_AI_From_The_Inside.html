Understanding AI from the Inside

Understanding AI from the Inside

An illustrated guide to “The State of Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety”

Looking inside an AI model can catch dangers that testing its answers will miss, but today’s tools still see only part of the picture, so they should be one layer of safety rather than the whole of it.

Based on the survey by Fazl Barez, Tung-Yu Wu and 25 co-authors from Oxford, Mila, EleutherAI, Tel Aviv University, MIT, Anthropic and other groups (arXiv 2611.08438, November 2026).

We build today’s AI systems by letting them learn from enormous amounts of text. People decide how a model learns, but not what it learns, so nobody can fully say how it reaches its answers. Mechanistic interpretability is the effort to open a model up and work out what is happening inside it, much as a biologist might trace the wiring of a brain. This survey asks a blunt question: does understanding a model’s insides actually make it safer? Their answer, roughly: sometimes, and never on its own. This page walks through why the question matters, the main ideas and tools, and where those tools help and where they fall short.

The page is meant to be read top to bottom, in six stops.

1

The problem

A gap that keeps widening

AI capabilities have improved enormously in recent years. Our ability to explain how models produce those capabilities has not kept pace. The authors call the difference the interpretability gap: the distance between what a model can do and what we understand about how it does it. As models grow, the gap can widen, leaving room for reasoning we can’t trace and behaviour we can’t anticipate.

Passing the test is not the same as being safe

The standard way to check a model is to watch what it outputs on a set of test questions. The survey lists three reasons this can mislead. Test questions leak into the training data, and benchmarks get “maxed out” long before the real skill is there. Two models can give identical answers for completely different internal reasons, one following a general rule and the other a brittle shortcut. And a model may learn to recognise when it is being evaluated and behave differently.

A model with a hidden switch

The sharpest example in the survey is the “sleeper agent” experiment. Researchers deliberately trained a model to write secure code whenever the prompt said the year was 2023, and to slip in security holes when it said 2024. They then applied the standard safety treatments: supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning from human feedback, and adversarial training. None removed the hidden behaviour. Adversarial training sometimes just taught the model to hide it better.

There is a hopeful twist. Follow-up work showed that a simple “probe”, a tiny classifier reading the model’s internal activity, could tell whether the hidden trigger was active almost perfectly, scoring above 99% on AUROC, the standard measure of how cleanly a detector separates two cases. Looking inside caught what watching outputs missed. But the authors draw a sober lesson as well: detecting a dangerous mechanism is not the same as removing it.

2

Core ideas

From what, to where, to how

Interpretability methods sit on a ladder of evidence. Behavioural tests tell you what a model does. Attribution and probing tell you where relevant information lives. Mechanistic methods aim to show how the computation produces the behaviour. The survey counts a method as mechanistic only if it works with the model’s internals and makes causal claims, which means changing something inside (switching a part off, swapping in different values, nudging it) and measuring what happens.

According to the authors, mechanistic understanding becomes genuinely necessary in four situations: uncovering intentions that never show up in outputs (deception, hidden side goals); explaining how a behaviour actually works; showing that a capability is absent, since tests can only confirm presence; and giving guarantees that must hold beyond the inputs anyone tested.

Neurons are crowded

A natural first guess is that each artificial neuron stands for one idea, such as “blue”. In practice many neurons respond to several unrelated things at once. This is called polysemanticity. A leading explanation is the superposition hypothesis: a model represents far more concepts than it has neurons, so it packs them in at angles that are almost, but not quite, perpendicular. Each neuron then ends up partly tuned to several concepts.

The survey is careful here: superposition is still a hypothesis in large language models, not an established fact. Polysemantic neurons can arise for other reasons too. Either way, the practical consequence is the same. Single neurons are often the wrong unit to study, so the field looks for features: patterns spread across many neurons that correspond to one concept and that, when nudged, change the behaviour linked to that concept.

Meaning lives in directions

The linear representation hypothesis says many concepts correspond to a direction in the model’s internal space. If that is true, you can measure how strongly a concept is present by checking how far the model’s internal state points along that direction, and you can strengthen or weaken the concept by adding or subtracting the direction. Probes, steering, and sparse autoencoders all rely on this. It works often enough to be useful, but the authors treat it as an approximation, not a law; some representations are curved, tangled across several subspaces, or only make sense as part of a computation.

Two more bets the field is making

Two further hypotheses shape the research. The privileged access hypothesis says a model might be able to report on its own internal states better than an outside observer could. There is some evidence: models sometimes notice concepts artificially injected into their activations. There are also negative results, such as models predicting their own sampling settings no better than another model’s. The universality hypothesis asks whether different models learn the same things. A weak version, where similar algorithms recur across models, has decent support. A strong version, where the internal representations themselves converge, looks fragile, which matters because it limits how far one model’s insights carry over to another.

One shared notebook: the residual stream

Most modern language models are transformers. One property makes them friendlier to study than you might expect. Information flows through a shared channel called the residual stream, and each layer reads from it and then adds its contribution back. Think of a notebook passed down a line of people, each reading it and writing a note at the bottom. Because contributions are added, researchers can ask how much each part wrote.

A circuit is then a small subgraph of this machinery: the particular blocks or features, and the connections between them, that together produce one behaviour. Circuits are the field’s main way of writing down a mechanism.

3

The toolkit

Untangling features with a sparse dictionary

If concepts are packed together in superposition, how do you pull them apart? The workhorse tool is the sparse autoencoder (SAE). It takes the model’s busy internal state, re-expresses it using a very large dictionary of possible features with the rule that only a handful may be active at once, and then rebuilds the original state from those few. The “only a few at once” rule pushes each dictionary entry towards meaning one thing. At scale, SAEs have pulled millions of features out of billion-parameter models.

SAEs come with a catalogue of failure modes, and the survey goes through them carefully. Some features never fire (“dead” features). Some appear twice. A general feature can be swallowed by a more specific one (absorption), so that a “starts with S” feature stays silent on “short” because a “short” feature fired instead. Training with a different random seed can produce a noticeably different dictionary. And many features that look meaningful turn out not to affect the model’s behaviour at all. Related tools stretch the same idea: transcoders learn to imitate a whole MLP block with sparse features, and cross-layer transcoders and crosscoders follow features across many layers, which makes tracing circuits easier. A different camp argues for designing models that are easier to read from the start, for example by forcing sparse weights.

Testing a part by swapping it out

To show that a part of the model causes a behaviour, researchers use activation patching. Run the model twice: once on a clean prompt and once on a slightly changed one. Then copy one component’s internal values from the second run into the first. If the answer flips, that component was carrying the information that differed.

Do this systematically and you can trace a circuit. The survey’s own illustration traces how a model completes “Dallas is in” with “Texas”: one feature recognises Dallas and links it to Texas, another registers that a state name should come next, and the two combine. Try knocking pieces out below.

Two cautions from the survey. Most circuit methods explain a single next word, and behaviours that unfold over long answers remain hard to trace. And a circuit that is sufficient on the test prompts may not be the whole story; models often have backup pathways that step in when one is removed.

Peeking at half-finished predictions

Another family of tools translates the middle of the model into words. The simplest, the logit lens, takes the internal state at some layer and asks: if the model had to answer right now, what would it say? Early layers usually give vague guesses that sharpen with depth. Below, the model sees the pattern “A B C A B” and should predict “C”.

Newer tools fix some of the logit lens’s blind spots. The tuned lens learns a small translator for each layer. Patchscopes ask the model itself to describe an internal state by inserting it into a different prompt. LatentQA fine-tunes a copy of the model to answer plain-language questions about another model’s activations. Probes remain popular too, with a long-standing warning attached: information that can be decoded from a layer is not necessarily information the model uses.

Steering with a single arrow

If concepts are directions, you can push a model along one. A steering vector is often built very simply: average the model’s internal state on prompts where it refuses, average it on prompts where it complies, and take the difference. Adding that arrow during a conversation pushes the model towards refusing; subtracting it pushes the other way. The same trick has shifted helpfulness, style and other traits, and it doubles as a causal test of what a probe has found.

In symbols the whole intervention is h̃ = h + α·v: take the internal state h and add α copies of the arrow v. Cheap and effective, but the survey warns that steering vectors tuned on one kind of input can fail, or even damage the model, when inputs change.

Watching a model learn

Most tools study a finished model. Another strand studies training itself, because abilities can appear suddenly. The classic case is grokking: on a small arithmetic task, a model memorises its training examples quickly, then sits on a long plateau, then abruptly starts getting new examples right. Looking inside showed that the general solution was being built gradually during the plateau, invisible in the scores, before the memorised shortcut was cleared away.

Sudden jumps matter for safety because they make it hard to predict what a model will be able to do from earlier checkpoints or smaller models. Internal “progress measures” offer a way to see a capability coming. A related toolset, training data attribution, works backwards from a behaviour to the training examples that most shaped it, either by searching for matching text or by estimating each example’s influence mathematically.

4

Checking explanations

Five questions every explanation should face

A recurring complaint in the survey is that interpretability findings are often accepted because they look plausible, and different methods report incompatible scores. The authors propose one framework with five dimensions. No single one is enough; a strong method should do well on all five.

Three easy mistakes

The survey names three tempting leaps that don’t hold:

An SAE rebuilds the activations well≠it captures the model’s computation
A circuit is sufficient on the test prompts≠the circuit is complete
A feature has a neat, human-readable label≠the model actually uses it

Even careful work should be read as the best current explanation, not the final word. The survey’s example: networks trained on group arithmetic were given one detailed explanation, which a second team showed was incomplete and replaced, after which a third team showed that both were incomplete and offered a broader account.

5

Safety

Six ways to do the research

The field has not converged on one approach. The authors map six research orientations, each trading off completeness, cost and practicality differently. None is best in general, and they can be combined.

Where the tools fit in a model’s life

The survey matches safety jobs to the methods that look most promising for each, and not all of them are mechanistic. During training, the aim is to stop dangerous abilities forming and encourage good ones like honesty. During evaluation, the aim is to catch problems such as reward hacking, planted backdoors or a model that behaves differently when it knows it’s being tested. In deployment, the aim is to watch for and correct bad behaviour as it happens.

The risk of seeing only part of the map

Interpretability has its own risks, and the survey sorts them into four kinds. Epistemic: false confidence from partial understanding. Operational: a tool that worked on test data quietly fails in the real world. Adversarial: published editing methods used to plant false knowledge in a model. Opportunity cost: effort diverted from simpler safeguards. The authors call the first the most insidious. Slide to see why.

There is also a longer-term worry. If interpretability tools are widely used, a capable enough model might learn to arrange its internals in ways that resist them, much as models have learned to spot evaluations. That could mean an arms race with no clear end.

6

The verdict

Questions still open

The authors argue that the most pressing gap is evaluation: shared benchmarks, including safety benchmarks, that let methods be compared fairly. Beyond that, several deeper questions remain. Will improving training methods eventually make looking inside less necessary? Does a label like “borzoi detector” describe something real, or a human choice among several equally valid descriptions? When a simpler stand-in model such as an SAE reproduces a model’s behaviour, does it reproduce its mechanism? And understanding a refusal circuit doesn’t tell you what a model should value; interpretability supports checking and control, but the question of specifying good behaviour is handled elsewhere.

One layer among several

The survey’s bottom line is explicit: mechanistic interpretability is not a cure-all for AI safety. It should be one part of a defence-in-depth strategy, used alongside behavioural testing, safer training and oversight, with its tools tested rigorously and their limits stated plainly.

The road ahead

The authors group future work into three complementary paradigms. Post-hoc explanation studies models after training, and will stay important because deployed models are not built to be readable. Learning dynamics studies how mechanisms form, so problems can be predicted and steered early. Interpretability by design builds architectures that are transparent from the start. Each feeds the others: what post-hoc work discovers can guide new designs, and what training studies reveal can suggest constraints.

Progress also depends on stronger scientific habits: claims that can be proven wrong and results others can reproduce. To help, the authors released code notebooks that reproduce key findings from the methods they survey, so readers can build intuition by experimenting directly.

So, does understanding how models work make them safer? The survey’s honest answer is that it can, especially for spotting what outputs hide, but only when its claims are tested hard, its blind spots are admitted, and it works alongside every other safeguard we have.
