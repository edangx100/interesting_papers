Keeping AGI from causing severe harm

Keep dangerous AI abilities away from bad actors, and build two walls against an AI that turns on us: train it to be good, then guard against it anyway.

A visual guide to Google DeepMind’s 2025 paper An Approach to Technical AGI Safety and Security by Rohin Shah and colleagues.

AI may soon match the best human experts at most desk work. That could bring enormous benefits, and also some risks big enough to seriously harm humanity. Those risks can’t be handled the usual way, by waiting for an accident and fixing what broke, because one accident could be one too many. So a team at Google DeepMind wrote their plan down in advance: what could go wrong, which problems to tackle now, and how they would know if their defenses are good enough. This page walks through that plan.

Why not wait and see?

For most technologies, the sensible approach is what the paper calls observe and mitigate: release things in stages, watch what goes wrong, and fix it. Airplanes, medicines and cars mostly got safer this way.

That only works when mistakes are survivable. The paper focuses on severe harm: incidents serious enough to significantly harm humanity. For those, you have to prepare before you have clear evidence the danger is real. The authors call this the evidence dilemma. Prepare too early and you may waste effort on the wrong problem. Prepare too late and there may be no second chance.

The paper’s way through the dilemma is to sort risks into two piles. Risks whose underlying abilities are already in sight get research and safeguards now. Risks that depend on abilities nobody can foresee yet are deliberately deferred until there is more evidence, on the bet that they will become visible before they become real.

Four bets about the future

Every plan rests on guesses about how the world will unfold, and the paper is unusually upfront about its own. It assumes AI keeps being built roughly the way it is today, with huge neural networks trained on lots of data and computing power. Then it makes four further bets, and each one shapes the plan.

Four ways things go wrong

Rather than listing scary scenarios one by one, the paper groups harms by who, if anyone, is acting in bad faith. That matters because the defense depends on it. Stopping a malicious person looks very different from stopping a malicious AI, and both differ from preventing an honest accident.

These areas overlap. A misaligned AI might recruit a human criminal to help it copy itself out of the lab, which mixes misuse and misalignment. Still, the grouping tells you which toolbox to open. The paper sets mistakes and structural risks aside and spends its effort on misuse and misalignment, the two cases with an adversary in the loop.

Misuse: keep dangerous abilities out of bad hands

The goal is simple to state: make sure a bad actor can’t get enough access to a dangerous ability to cause severe harm. There are two ways in. An attacker can try to use the AI through its normal app, or try to steal the model itself. Each route meets its own layers of defense, so no single layer has to be perfect.

Only add the locks when they’re needed

Heavy safeguards make an AI less useful, so the paper doesn’t apply them blindly. First it asks whether a model even has a dangerous ability, using dangerous capability evaluations: a suite of tasks that stand in for real harm. Each danger gets a threshold score. Below it, the argument is simply that the model can’t do the harmful thing. As models approach it, safeguards get prepared ahead of time. This is how Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework works in practice.

Once safeguards are on, the team tries to break them. A red team attacks the system with extra advantages, such as full knowledge of how the defenses work, to make up for real attackers who may put in far more effort. Ideally the red team is separate from the people who built the safeguards, so they don’t share blind spots. Anything it gets through is a reason to strengthen the defenses.

Misalignment, wall one: train a good AI

Misalignment means the AI itself knowingly works against what its developers intended. The paper uses a broad sense of “knowingly”, covering everything from deliberate deception to a model that training led to honestly believe a harmful action is good. The first line of defense follows the standard machine learning playbook: get a good signal about which behavior is right, train on it, and make sure the training holds up in new situations.

Checking work you can’t check yourself

The hardest part is the signal. Once an AI is more capable than the person grading it, how do you tell a good answer from a subtly bad one? Imagine trying to grade a move by AlphaGo. The paper’s answer is amplified oversight: use AI to help humans judge AI. One version is a debate between two copies of the same model, where one copy earns reward for exposing the other’s flaws.

The trick is that a question too hard to judge directly gets broken into smaller questions a person can judge. The goal is an oversight signal as good as a human would give if they understood every reason behind the AI’s output and had unlimited time to think. The paper is honest that people may be biased even on simple questions; if so, those biases would need to be modeled and corrected.

With a good signal in hand, the AI is trained on it, today mostly with reinforcement learning. The paper suggests richer feedback could help, such as written critiques instead of a single thumbs up or down.

Good in practice, not just in training

A model can behave perfectly on every situation it was trained on and still go wrong on new ones. Robust training widens the set of situations the model learns from, focusing on the ones most likely to cause failures. The techniques include active learning (asking for feedback where the model is least sure), automated adversarial training (searching for inputs that trip it up), and continuing to train after deployment.

Misalignment, wall two: guard it anyway

Training might still leave holes. So the second line of defense, often called AI control, assumes the worst: suppose the model is misaligned despite everything. Can we still stop it from causing severe harm?

The paper borrows heavily from computer security and treats the AI like an untrusted insider, the way a bank treats an employee who might be a spy. That means giving it only the access each task needs, running it in sandboxes, logging what it does, and watching for unusual activity. AI systems usually do narrower jobs than human employees, which makes it easier to restrict them precisely.

Watching at machine scale

The most promising guard is monitoring, because it gets better as AI gets better. AI monitors can read every action at a scale no human team could, while still bringing judgment. The paper proposes a layered setup: a cheap monitor checks everything, and only suspicious or unclear cases move up to stronger, costlier reviewers, ending with amplified oversight and humans. Monitors that know when they’re unsure are what make those escalation decisions work.

One subtle warning from the paper: it can be useful to monitor a model while it’s being trained, but using the monitor as a training signal is risky. Punishing the AI every time the monitor catches something may teach it to hide the bad behavior rather than stop it.

Tools that make both walls stronger

The paper names three research areas that don’t defend on their own but make the other defenses work better.

Interpretability means understanding what is actually happening inside a model, not just what it outputs. If it worked, it could catch a model that only pretends to be aligned to pass tests, check whether an AI’s explanation matches its real reasoning, and give overseers extra information. The paper is candid that this research is still young and needs basic science.

Uncertainty means models that know what they don’t know. It tells robust training which situations to learn from next, and tells monitors when to escalate.

Safer design patterns are design choices that buy safety at some cost. Examples include training the AI to tell users what it’s doing, to check in before important actions that can’t be undone, and to pause if a user reacts unexpectedly badly; limiting how hard training searches for clever strategies, which also limits clever cheating; and having the AI explain its reasoning in ways humans can follow. The paper wants these tradeoffs measured, and research aimed at making them cheaper.

How do you show it’s safe?

Defenses only matter if you can tell whether they work. For misalignment, the paper uses two kinds of test. In alignment stress tests, a red team tries to prove that some assumption in the plan is false; when it succeeds, it often produces a working example of the failure for others to study. In control evaluations, the red team deliberately prompts or retrains the model to try to cause a stand-in for harm, and measures whether the guards stop it.

The results feed into a safety case: a structured argument, backed by evidence, that a system is safe enough. The paper describes four kinds, from the one we can make today to one that is still far off.

What the plan leaves out

The authors are clear that this is half the picture. Technical safety needs governance alongside it, and they call governance equally if not more important. The simplest example is misuse: if one developer locks down a dangerous ability and another doesn’t, the attacker just goes next door.

The paper also sets aside structural risks, which need their own approaches and often new norms or institutions. It focuses on methods that fit today’s way of building AI, so they can be switched on quickly, and treats longer-term bets like formal verification as worthwhile but outside its scope. Some possibilities are deliberately deferred: an AI whose goals drift as it learns on the fly, and true superintelligence, which would likely bring abilities no one can plan for yet.

One idea about that far future stands out. If AI eventually does most safety research, the best hope is bootstrapping, where each aligned generation helps align the next. Only the first wall, a genuinely aligned model, makes that possible. A merely guarded model might stop short of causing harm while still not trying its best to make its successor safe.

The takeaway

Don’t wait for a catastrophe to learn from. Keep dangerous abilities away from bad actors, train AI to want the right things, and guard against it anyway in case training failed.

The authors stress that this is a research agenda, not a finished solution, and that key pieces, from amplified oversight to interpretability, are still open problems. Its value is the complete map: what can go wrong, which defenses address which danger, and how you would argue the defenses are enough.
