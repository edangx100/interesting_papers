Who makes the agent’s next move?

Most steps an AI agent takes are really multiple-choice picks, and this paper shows that a small, fast “decision model” can make most of them, cutting cost by more than 80% without hurting success, as long as it hands the uncertain ones back to the big language model.

An illustrated explainer of JevAgentHarness: Benchmarking, Deploying and Training Decision Models That Share Control with LLMs by Kunlun Zhu, Tao Feng, Xu Han, Heng Wang, Ling Yang, Xiangru Tang, Jiaxuan You and Heng Ji (2026). Read the paper

AI agents work through a task one step at a time. They read a customer’s message, look something up, take an action, check the result, and go around again. Today a large language model is asked to think at every one of those steps, which makes long tasks slow and expensive. Look closely, though, and most steps end with a simple pick from a short list: which tool to use next, which value to fill in, whether the job is finished. This paper asks a practical question. Can a much smaller model make those picks instead, and how would we know when to trust it?

Every step is a multiple-choice question

The program that runs an agent is called its harness. At each step it shows the model the task, what has happened so far and the latest result, then asks something like “which tool should the agent call next?” The paper’s test world, called DecisionGym, is a customer-service desk with 69 tools. Here is one of its tasks, cut down to six tools: a customer wants a pending order shipped, express if they’re a gold-tier customer, but forgot to say who they are.

A decision model doesn’t write an answer. It returns a probability for every option at once. Step through the episode and watch the bars.

Look at the very first decision. The customer gave no name, the small model wasn’t sure what to do, and its top option stayed below the line, so the LLM took that step. For the next four it was confident, and each answer cost one quick pass of a small network instead of a full LLM call. That handoff is the whole idea of the paper.

Two kinds of minds

An LLM reaches a decision by writing: it generates text token by token until a tool call comes out the other end. A decision model reads the situation once and returns a probability for each listed option, without generating any text. The commercial decision model the paper builds on is called Jev. The authors also train their own open one, and it is cheap to run: on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU it answers in a median of 31 milliseconds and costs about $0.0045 per thousand decisions, roughly a quarter of Jev’s price per decision.

Good at quizzes, worse at the job

To see whether decision models can actually run an agent, the authors built AgentDecisionBench. Its key design choice is to test the same models at three levels of realism, from a quiz to the real job.

The headline finding is that a good quiz score didn’t predict a good agent. The decision model with the best score on isolated tool-choice questions solved only 69.5% of agent tasks. And when tool choices were simply handed over with no confidence threshold, task success fell by 19.0 points.

Why would a model that answers questions well fail at running an agent? The paper’s results point to two reasons, and both are easier to see than to state.

Small slips compound

An agent makes many decisions in a row, and in the live setting one bad pick can derail everything after it. Here is a deliberately simple model of that, not taken from the paper: if each decision is right with probability p, a run of n decisions with zero mistakes happens with probability pn. The states nobody practises

The second reason is subtler. Quiz questions, and most training data, come from tidy situations. A running agent that has just made a mistake ends up somewhere unusual: a wrong lookup, a confused customer, a half-finished order. Existing decision models had rarely, if ever, seen states like these, so they were weakest exactly when the agent most needed a steady hand.

The fix: a confidence gate

These findings shape the paper’s harness, JevAgentHarness. For each kind of choice, such as picking a tool or filling in an argument, it sets a confidence threshold τ. When the decision model’s confidence is at or above τ, its answer is used. Below it, the LLM decides as it always would. The decision model only takes the turns it is sure about.

Where the line sits is a trade-off. Too low, and confident mistakes slip through. Too high, and the LLM does nearly all the work, so nothing is saved. Drag the gate and watch both numbers move.

How do you find the right τ without risking real tasks? The harness runs the decision model in shadow mode first. The LLM keeps full control while the small model quietly answers every question it would have been asked, and its answers are logged. Comparing the log with what actually worked gives a threshold fitted to this agent, these tools and this kind of task.

The fitted values are revealing. For AgentDecision-4B working alongside Gemini 3.5 Flash, the gate for choosing a tool sat at 0.445, while the gate for filling in an argument value sat far higher, at 0.996. The authors stress that these numbers only hold for the setting they were fitted in. A different LLM, different tools or a different task family means running shadow mode again.

Training a model on its own mistakes

Since existing decision models never saw the states that follow an agent’s errors, the authors trained one that has. AgentDecision-4B is a small adapter on top of the open Qwen3-4B model, built in three stages.

One detail in the first stage is worth seeing. Rather than training only on the single correct answer, the model learns a blend: mostly the teacher’s full spread of probabilities, plus some weight on the correct answer. That way it also learns how unsure to be, which matters a lot when a confidence gate is going to read its probabilities.

Two honest notes from the paper. The reinforcement-learning stage added no measurable gain in task success over the first stage alone. And the final pass on public data raised the model’s quiz score from 78.8% to 85.1% while lowering its success on shifted tasks from 96.0% to 91.5%: the same quiz-versus-job tension, showing up inside one model.

What it bought

Put the pieces together, a decision model trained on agent states and a gate fitted in shadow mode, and the agent got much cheaper and faster without getting worse. On 200 new DecisionGym tasks, with Gemini 3.5 Flash as the LLM and AgentDecision-4B on its own GPU, this is how the harness compared with the LLM working alone.

The savings come from how much the small model ended up handling. In a test registered before it was run, on 500 new tasks, the earlier version of the model made 99.9% of tool choices and 75.0% of argument choices, saving about 4.5 LLM calls per task. And on unfamiliar, shifted tasks where the decision model made every tool choice with no gate at all, the agent solved 91.5% of them with AgentDecision-4B against 82.0% with Jev, the very teacher it learned from.

Where it breaks

The paper is unusually direct about its limits, and they matter for anyone tempted to deploy this.

A gate fitted on familiar work doesn’t protect you on unfamiliar work. On shifted tasks, the thresholds fitted on the original tasks still let the model take every tool choice, including 22 wrong ones, and success dropped 7.5 points below the LLM alone. Confidence that was trustworthy at home was overconfident abroad.

New rules are a cliff, not a slope. Given an order rule it had never trained on, the model still made most of the cancel-or-ship decisions, got too many wrong, and the agent solved almost nothing. Retraining on the rule largely fixed the accuracy, which says the approach works but needs data from the actual job.

Irreversible steps need a second check. A confident wrong answer at a step that can’t be undone, like cancelling an order when the agent should have asked first, ends the task. The authors’ advice is plain: never make a decision model the only safeguard on an irreversible action.

The scope is narrow so far. Everything was trained and tested on English customer-service tasks with one family of LLMs. Coding agents weren’t validated, and the models weren’t tested against adversarial input such as instructions hidden in tool output.

The bigger lesson

The practical recipe is simple to state. Let a small model handle the routine picks, gate it with a threshold fitted on the agent’s own runs, and let the LLM take everything it isn’t sure about. The deeper point is about measurement. A decision model’s quiz score said little about how it would run an agent; what mattered was how it behaved inside the loop, on the messy states that real agents actually visit. Test and train it there, and a 4B model can carry most of the load for a fraction of the cost.
