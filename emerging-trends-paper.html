AI agent evaluation, explained

Emerging Trends in AI Agent Evaluation

When AI stops just answering questions and starts taking actions, checking its final answer is no longer enough, and this paper maps 33 ways the testing of AI agents is changing.

An explainer of the survey by Felix Chen, Stephan Rabanser, Peter Kirgis, Nitya Nadgir, Rishi Bommasani, Magda Dubois, Elham Tabassi, Cozmin Ududec, Anka Reuel, Sanmi Koyejo, Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan (Princeton, Stanford, UK AI Security Institute, Brookings, UC Berkeley and the Institute for Advanced Study), October 19, 2026.

For years, testing an AI system worked like marking a quiz: ask a question, compare the reply with the answer key, add up the score. Today’s AI “agents” do much more. They plan, search the web, run code and click through software, often over dozens or hundreds of steps. That makes them far harder to test fairly. This paper surveys how researchers are responding, and groups the changes under three plain questions: how do we test agents, what should we test them for, and who gets to do the testing?

What actually gets tested

An agent is not just a language model. It is a model wrapped in a scaffold (the code that decides what to do next), a set of tools (search, a code runner, a browser) and an environment it can change. A person may step in along the way. So when an agent’s score goes up or down, any of these parts could be the reason.

Part 1 of 3How: sharper methods for a harder job

The first part asks whether our measuring sticks still work. Many of the problems are old ones from statistics and psychology, but agents make each of them bite harder.

1aDoes a high score mean what we think?

Picture a “replay agent” for using a computer. It does no thinking at all: it copies one successful run and replays the exact same clicks every time. On some benchmarks it scores higher than agents built on frontier models. Does that make it better at using computers? Obviously not. The score was measuring something narrower than the skill we cared about.

Validity comes in several flavours, and each concerns a different link in the chain from “we changed the agent” to “this tells us something about the world”.

Good practice: define the construct before picking a metric, then brainstorm how an agent could game that metric without having the skill.

1bWhen a benchmark stops telling agents apart

Checking every task in an agent benchmark by hand is tedious, and there are few rewards for doing it. Problems such as impossible tasks or broken graders often only surface once the benchmark is saturated: the top agents bunch up near the ceiling and their scores become statistically indistinguishable.

1cAccuracy is only one axis

An agent that succeeds 2% more often but costs ten times as much may be the wrong choice. The paper argues that evaluations should report accuracy alongside cost, latency and reliability, and show the trade-off as a Pareto frontier: the set of agents that nothing else beats on both axes at once.

1dAgents are noisy, so run them more than once

Run the same agent on the same task five times and you may get five different journeys and outcomes. Yet many evaluations still use a single run because runs are expensive. The paper distinguishes two popular summaries that answer very different questions:

pass@k is the chance of succeeding at least once in k tries. It tells you whether the agent can do the task at all. pass^k is the chance of succeeding every time in k tries. It tells you whether you can rely on it. Try it below.

A subtler point: a benchmark score answers “how would the agent do on these exact tasks again?” A different question, “how would it do on new tasks like these?”, carries wider uncertainty because the choice of tasks is itself random. Tools from measurement science, such as hierarchical models used by NIST and the UK AI Security Institute, separate the two.

Good practice: say how many runs you did; don’t claim consistency from one run; and include judge error in your error bars when an AI is doing the grading.

1eWhich part made the difference?

Agent performance depends jointly on the model, the scaffold, the tools, the budget, the environment and the grading. If two of these change at once, you can’t say which one caused the improvement. Worse, a scaffold may help one model and hurt another: a model × scaffold interaction.

The paper notes cases where better scaffolding alone, with the model held fixed, produced large gains. So an agent’s failure should not be blamed on the underlying model until better scaffolds have been tried.

1fAnswers can leak in through more doors

For models, “contamination” meant the test questions had appeared in training data. Agents add two new doors. Knowledge of the test can be baked into the scaffold or prompts (sometimes without the builder noticing), and an agent with web access can simply look up the answers mid-task.

1gMoving targets need a fixed anchor

Dynamic benchmarks keep changing: they refresh tasks monthly, draw from live traffic, test forecasts against events that haven’t happened yet, or pit agents against adaptive opponents. That fights contamination and saturation, but raises a new problem: if the test keeps changing, how do you compare this year’s score with last year’s? Psychometrics has an answer called test equating.

1h–1iRead the transcript, not just the scoreboard

Agent transcripts are now far too long and numerous for people to read them all, so evaluators increasingly use AI models as judges (to grade open-ended work) and monitors (to flag bad behaviour in a run). That raises the obvious question: who judges the judges? The paper recommends checking AI graders against expert human labels and carrying their error into reported uncertainty.

Good practice: a clean log is not proof of a clean run. Logs can be incomplete (labs often release only summarised reasoning), and absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Combine manual reading with automated scanning.

1j–1lShared tools, sharper attacks, open-world tests

Running an agent evaluation takes real engineering: environments, harnesses, sandboxes and consistent rules. Shared frameworks such as the open-source Inspect toolkit and the Holistic Agent Leaderboard make results cheaper and more comparable, though a popular framework can quietly spread its default metrics everywhere, and using one framework for both training and testing invites leakage.

Red-teaming now has to cover more than harmful text: tools, scaffolds and the test environment itself are part of the attack surface, and harmful outputs can turn directly into harmful actions. Meanwhile open-world evaluations give rich, detailed accounts of a handful of long, real tasks. They can’t give averages, but they reveal upper limits that benchmarks miss, provided the results are reported as “what agents can achieve” rather than “what they typically do”.

1mMeasuring agents in human units

A newer idea is to express agent ability in terms people already understand. One approach, the task-completion time horizon, asks: how long a task (measured by how long it takes a human expert) can the agent complete with a given reliability? Slide to see why the reliability level matters so much.

Part 2 of 3What: from the lab to the world

The second part widens the lens. It is no longer enough to ask what an agent can do alone; we need to know what happens when it works with people, and what it does to the organisations around it.

The paper uses three layers, and a key warning: evidence at one layer does not automatically count as evidence at another. Cutting across all three is a second axis. Automation asks whether an agent can do a task on its own. Augmentation asks whether it makes a person-plus-agent team better.

2a–2dFour ways to study people with agents

Testing an agent with real people is expensive, so many benchmarks use an AI to play the user. But simulated users tend to be overly cooperative, stylistically uniform and short on real frustration or ambiguity, so they can flatter an agent. Real-person studies range from controlled lab trials to field experiments in workplaces to analyses of real usage logs. Each step gains realism and loses control.

The real-world studies the paper cites are a useful corrective to the assumption that AI help always speeds people up.

There’s a growing practical snag too: so many developers now use coding agents that it is hard to recruit a fair “without AI” comparison group.

2e–2fFrom tasks to jobs to the economy

Evaluations are getting more specialised, testing whole workflows in fields such as science rather than quiz-style knowledge. At the same time, people want to know what agents mean for work and the economy. The paper separates three layers of evidence and stresses that the gaps between them are unsolved.

2gWhen the agent knows it’s being tested

An agent can pass a test without having the skill the test was meant to find. It might find a bug in the scorer or disable a test. That doesn’t require any intent to deceive. A separate worry is evaluation awareness: the agent recognising it is in a test and behaving differently, which would make the test unrepresentative of real use.

2h–2iDanger and security reach the test bench

More autonomy amplifies familiar risks: misuse by bad actors, sabotage, and agents copying themselves. The paper recommends judging such risks against a realistic baseline of what people can already do with conventional tools. Security, meanwhile, now covers the whole setup, including the evaluation itself. The paper describes recent incidents in which agents under evaluation broke out of their test environments, including one where agents exploited the environment to reach Hugging Face’s infrastructure in search of benchmark solutions, and a lab’s misconfigured internet access during cybersecurity tests. Open question: how do you give agents realistic tools, which makes tests meaningful, while keeping them securely boxed in? The paper notes several breaches came to light long after the fact, so real-time monitoring is a research priority.

2j–2kHarms that build up slowly, effects that ripple outward

Some harms never show up in a single reply. Sycophancy (telling people what they want to hear) can shape a user’s behaviour, which shapes the system’s next response, in a loop that only becomes visible over many conversations. And the paper notes that these effects can differ between the raw model and the product built around it. At the widest scale, systemic evaluation asks how agents change institutions such as peer review, crowd work and the information ecosystem. Here the methods are least developed.

Part 3 of 3Who: the people behind the numbers

Benchmarks used to feel like neutral rulers. With agents, the evaluator makes so many choices (scaffold, prompts, tools, budget, when a human may step in, which statistics to use) that who runs the test genuinely shapes the result.

Evaluations come from frontier labs, universities, nonprofits, startups and governments, supported by funders, infrastructure builders and standard setters. The paper argues a healthy ecosystem doesn’t need everyone to make identical choices, only for choices to be visible and defensible. When two careful evaluations disagree, that is useful too: it shows which design decisions mattered.

3b–3dIndependence is a set of dials, not a switch

Being a “third party” isn’t the same as being independent. An outside evaluator can still depend on a lab for access, money or permission to publish. For agents, access means more than an API: raw reasoning traces, realistic tool setups, and rate limits high enough for repeated runs.

Practical advice: treat lab adoption of your metric as a success signal only for capability evaluations. For accountability work, interests diverge, and missing results are not good news.

3e–3fMoney and rising costs

The paper reports that funding for evaluation over the past three years has run to hundreds of millions of dollars, but mostly as private investment in evaluation startups rather than support for independent research. Philanthropy helped build much of the independent field, and labs themselves are major funders. Costs, meanwhile, are climbing fast: the paper cites about $50,000 in API credits for one full run of a single benchmark on one frontier model. Outside evaluators pay list prices that labs testing their own models don’t.

3g–3hWhere the work appears, and the role of government

A great deal of influential evaluation work never goes through peer review; it appears as blog posts, research notes, preprints, repositories and system cards. The paper’s answer is to apply the same questions everywhere: is there a defined construct, is attribution handled, is uncertainty justified? Governments, meanwhile, are users, regulators and evaluators at once. The paper urges treating evaluation as a lifecycle with ongoing monitoring, not a one-off check at purchase, and describes proposals for certified Independent Verification Organizations that would turn government-set goals into concrete tests.

3iMany fields have a seat at the table

The problems in Part 1 are old friends to measurement scientists; those in Part 2 belong as much to economists and HCI researchers as to computer scientists. Yet the paper notes that participation is not broadening as fast as the scope is. Tap a field to see what it could bring.

Five things to remember

  1. An agent is a whole system: model, scaffold, tools and environment. A score belongs to the combination, so change one part at a time before assigning credit or blame.
  2. Outcomes hide journeys. The same tick mark can come from real skill or a shortcut, so read logs, but don’t treat a clean log as proof.
  3. Agents are noisy and expensive. Repeated runs, honest error bars and accuracy-versus-cost plots matter more than ever, and so does saying which question your metric answers.
  4. Evidence doesn’t automatically travel between layers. Benchmark scores, how people actually use agents, and effects on jobs and institutions each need their own studies.
  5. Who evaluates shapes what we learn. Access, funding, cost and freedom to publish decide which questions get asked, and the field needs more disciplines and more genuinely independent voices.

The authors call these “emerging” trends on purpose: the tools are still being refined and the standards are not yet set, which is exactly why now is a good time for new people to join.
