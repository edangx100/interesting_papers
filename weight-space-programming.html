LLMs can read and write neural network weights

Handed nothing but a network’s raw numbers, a frontier language model can say what the network does, repair what’s broken inside it, and write new working networks from scratch, all without any training.

An illustrated guide to “Sparks of Weight-Space Programming” by Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab, MIT CSAIL, November 2026.

Every neural network is, underneath, a long list of numbers called weights. Nobody writes those numbers by hand. Instead, we show the network a mountain of examples and let an algorithm nudge every number a tiny bit, over and over, until the network behaves. The finished list is famously unreadable: staring at it tells a person about as much as staring at the grooves of a vinyl record tells you the song.

This work reports that a large language model can look at the grooves and hear the music. It can also cut new grooves.

First, what is a weight?

Picture a network as dots in columns, joined by lines. Each line takes the signal from one dot, multiplies it by a number, and passes it to the next. That number is the line’s weight. Large positive weights carry a signal along strongly, negative ones push against it, and weights near zero barely matter.

Flip the figure to see what the language model actually receives: no dots and no lines, just the numbers written out as text. Its job is to work backwards from that text to behavior, or forwards from a goal to the text.

Two ways to get good weights

The standard method is gradient descent. Run the network, measure how wrong it is, work out which way to nudge each weight, nudge, and repeat, often hundreds of millions of times. The rule that does the nudging (an optimizer such as Adam) is a small fixed formula. It has no idea what the network is for.

The paper calls the alternative weight-space programming. A general-purpose language model is told the goal in ordinary words, reasons about what each part of the network computes or ought to compute, then writes or edits the numbers itself. No gradients and no training loop. In several experiments it never even gets to run the network.

The model doing this is GPT-6 Astra, used as is, with no special training for the job. The authors frame the results as early sparks rather than a finished method, and they come in three flavors.

Reading: what does this network do?

In the first experiments the authors train a network themselves, give Astra only its weights, and ask what it was trained for.

Which game does it play? Experiment 1

Four small networks learned to play grid versions of Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man and Freeway. All four have the same shape, the same inputs and the same five actions, so the only clue is in their 17,253 numbers. Astra wasn’t told which four games were in play, yet it named the right one every time.

What shape does it draw? Experiment 2

Next came three tiny image generators (diffusion models) trained to produce filled circles, squares or triangles. With no tools and no permission to run them, Astra went to each model’s final layer, where every hidden unit paints one stretch of the shape’s edge.

It got all three right, with a telling slip: it drew outlines, while the real models produce solid shapes. Simulating the network step by step would have revealed the fill. The mistake suggests Astra was reading the geometry of the weights, not quietly running the math.

What did this language model learn, from its middle alone? Experiment 3

First and last layers are the easy part, since they sit right next to the data. So the authors trained three tiny language models, one on Python code, one on Japanese written in the Latin alphabet (romaji), one on English, and then cut off the input and output layers. What was left were four transformer blocks with nothing to say which number stood for which letter. Astra could analyze them with Python but was forbidden from running any of it.

It named the right language for all three, in both of its attempts. Along the way it found every copying head: a part of the network that spots a chunk of text it has seen before and predicts that whatever followed last time will follow again.

Its clues were sensible. Code reuses names constantly, so a crowd of copying heads pointed to Python. Romaji never uses letters such as q, l, v or x, and the last block of that model wrote into noticeably fewer directions, as if it had fewer letters to keep apart. To find the copying heads, Astra multiplied weight matrices together, rediscovering a known interpretability technique nobody had mentioned to it.

Which chatbot is a flatterer? Experiment 7

Two copies of a small Gemma model were fine-tuned the same way, except that one learned to give correct answers and the other learned to agree with whatever answer the user suggested. Could Astra spot the sycophant?

From weights alone, no. Across four tries it found plenty of differences but couldn’t tie them to behavior. Given one extra hint, where each attention head looks while the models read text Astra wrote (still never seeing their answers), it picked out the sycophant in all three pairs.

Fixing: repair the weights by hand

The car that wouldn’t back up Experiment 4

In a 2D physics game, a car must reach a blue goal by jumping a red strip. The trick is to reverse first for a running start. A network trained with standard reinforcement learning for a billion steps never figured that out: it drives straight at the ramp and stalls, every single time.

Astra got the weights, a description of the scene and one recording of the failure. In a single reply, with no tools, it diagnosed the problem as a local optimum and wired in a small side path through a few units in each layer. That path reads the car’s position and speed, orders a reverse-then-charge, and switches itself off once the car is past the strip, handing control back to the original network.

Making a real language model better at math Experiment 8

Now a real, if small, language model: Qwen3.5-0.8B, with 800 million weights. That’s far too many to paste into a prompt, so Astra got a Python notebook over the weights (no GPU), practice problems, and a small budget of forward passes, meaning chances to run the model on a batch of inputs. Training and gradients were off limits.

Astra noticed the model kept getting stuck in loops, repeating itself until it ran out of room: 85 of its first 96 practice answers hit the 32,768-token ceiling. Qwen’s own documentation warns about this habit. Astra traced the looping to a single direction in the model’s internal space, written by the MLP blocks in layers 15 and 19, and subtracted that direction out.

Where it stopped working. The authors tried the same thing on Gemma 3 270M, a model that rarely loops. Astra found only ordinary mistakes, like bad arithmetic, with no single culprit inside the weights, and its edits moved the math score by about a point at most. Editing could clear away an obstacle that was hiding an ability. It couldn’t conjure an ability that wasn’t there.

Writing: build a network from nothing

Three arcade games, zero training Experiment 5

For three games from the MinAtar suite (Breakout, Seaquest and Asterix), Astra got the rules, the game’s source code and an empty network with every weight set to zero. No data, no gradients, no tools, no test runs. It wrote every weight as text in one reply, and only then was the network allowed to play.

Its method was to decide what program the network should run, then express that program as weights. For Breakout, the first layer works out where the ball will land and the second moves the paddle there.

A Go player, written as a program Experiment 6

Go on a 9×9 board calls for a much larger network: 1.48 million weights, too many to type out. Astra worked in a Python sandbox where it could build the weight tensors with numpy but couldn’t run the network, except by spending one of a limited number of forward passes. Training and game-tree search were banned.

With zero forward passes (a single Python run, 336 lines long) its network won more than half its games against two smaller trained networks. Given 50 forward passes to play practice games, study its losses and patch the part responsible, it beat a network of the same size trained by PPO on 20 million games.

What to keep in mind

This is a blog post reporting early experiments, not a peer-reviewed paper, and most of the networks are tiny by modern standards. Context length is a hard limit: once a network no longer fits in the prompt, the model needs tools and a harness, and the authors describe theirs as still crude. Reading weights alone failed the sycophancy test, and editing couldn’t teach Gemma math it didn’t already know.

Why it could matter

If language models can translate between plain language and weights, several things change.

Auditing without testing. Hidden problems, a planted backdoor for example, could be found by reading the weights, instead of hoping some test happens to trigger them.

Cheaper optimization. A decent network could be written in one shot rather than built by collecting data and running long training jobs.

Surgical edits. Adding or removing a capability might mean touching a sliver of the weights, like the 0.016% in the Qwen fix, leaving the rest of the model and what it knows undisturbed.

Self-improvement. A model that can inspect and debug its own weights has a far more direct route to improving itself than writing training scripts.

Real model merging. Today’s merging mostly averages models of identical shape. A model that understands weights could transplant a circuit from one architecture into a different one.

The authors are building a benchmark and harness to measure this skill, and they’ve published complete logs of every experiment, including every prompt, reply and line of code.
