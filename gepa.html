PAPER EXPLAINER

Teaching an AI system by rewriting its instructions

GEPA improves an AI system by reading its own failed attempts in plain English and editing the instructions it was given — reaching better results than reinforcement learning while needing up to 35 times fewer attempts.

On GEPA: Reflective Prompt Evolution Can Outperform Reinforcement Learning — Agrawal et al., arXiv:2507.19457

Suppose you have an assistant that answers hard research questions by searching, reading, and writing a summary. It gets about four in ten right. You want it better.

The standard fix is to retrain the model: let it attempt the task thousands of times, score each attempt, and slowly adjust its internal settings toward whatever earned higher scores. It works. It is also slow and expensive, because every attempt costs a real search, a real model call, and real money.

The authors of this paper noticed something plain. When the assistant fails, it does not fail silently. It leaves behind a complete written record — what it searched for, what came back, what it reasoned, and often an explicit note from the grader saying which document it missed. That record is in English. Models are good at English. So instead of crushing all of it into a single number, why not hand the record to a model and ask it what went wrong?

That is the whole idea. The system that does it is called GEPA, and it changes nothing inside the model. It only rewrites the instructions.

The cost problem

An attempt — the paper calls it a rollout — is one full run of the system on one example, plus scoring. Retraining methods like GRPO typically burn tens of thousands of these. GEPA finished the same job on one benchmark after a few hundred, and scored higher.

Across six benchmarks the pattern held: on average GEPA beat GRPO by about 6 points, and on the best case by around 20. The shape of the learning is different too. Retraining creeps upward across the whole budget. Prompt rewriting jumps early, because a single good edit changes the system's behaviour on every future example at once.

What is actually being edited

These assistants are rarely one model call. They are small pipelines: one step writes a search query, a tool fetches documents, another step summarises them, another writes the final answer. Each step is a model working from its own short written instruction.

Those instructions are the only thing GEPA touches. The model's weights stay frozen — which is why this works on models you cannot retrain at all, such as ones behind an API.

A number versus a sentence

Here is the difference the paper is really arguing about. A run that fails produces two things: a rich written trail, and a score. Reinforcement learning keeps the score and discards the trail. It then has to infer, from many scores, which part of the behaviour was at fault.

GEPA keeps both. The score still decides whether an edit survives. The trail is what tells it what to edit.

One turn of the loop

GEPA keeps a pool of candidate instruction-sets, starting with just the original one. It then repeats four steps until the budget runs out.

Each new candidate is a copy of its parent with one instruction changed, so lessons stack up along a lineage. A prompt that started as one line can end up as a page of task-specific advice, each paragraph traceable to a failure that produced it.

STARTING INSTRUCTION, SECOND-HOP QUERY WRITER Given the fields question, summary_1, produce the fields query.
AFTER OPTIMISATION — PARAPHRASED SUMMARY OF WHAT GEPA ADDED An explanation of what a second hop is for; a warning not to paraphrase the original question or repeat what the first hop already found; the observation that first-hop documents usually cover one entity while the missing document tends to cover a broader connected one; a worked example about a parish population versus an archipelago population; and a procedure for spotting the hinted-at entity and querying it directly.

None of that was written by a person. It was inferred from a handful of failures.

Why it does not get stuck

The obvious way to run this loop is to always improve whatever currently scores highest. The paper shows that this fails in a specific way: you find one good idea, then spend the rest of the budget failing to improve it. The search becomes a stalled chain.

GEPA's answer is to stop asking "which candidate is best overall" and ask "which candidate is best at something". For every single validation example, it records the highest score any candidate has ever achieved. A candidate that holds the record on even one example is kept, however mediocre its average.

The effect on the search is visible. Greedy selection produces a long thin chain of failed refinements. Sampling across the winners produces a bushy tree that explores several distinct strategies and finds a better one within the same budget.

Merging two lineages

Because different branches often improve different steps of the pipeline, GEPA can occasionally combine them: take the best version of each step from two cousins that share an ancestor and improved disjoint parts. The paper calls this Merge, and it is deliberately rare — both parents must have genuinely improved on the ancestor, and in different places.

Three further findings are worth pulling out.

Written rules beat worked examples. The previous best optimiser, MIPROv2, spends most of its prompt budget on few-shot demonstrations. GEPA writes instructions instead — and its prompts came out up to 9.2 times shorter while scoring higher. Shorter prompts are also cheaper to run forever afterwards, since every request pays for those tokens.

The prompts travel. Instructions evolved entirely on the small open model, then dropped unchanged into GPT-4.1 mini, gained 9 points — beating every optimiser that had been tuned directly on GPT-4.1 mini.

It is cheap. Every GPT-4.1 mini experiment in the paper cost under $500 in total; GEPA's own share was $86.

Two side uses

If you feed GEPA the very problems you want solved rather than a training set, it stops generalising and starts grinding: it overfits to those problems, carrying lessons from one across to the others. Used that way on GPU and NPU kernel generation, with compiler errors fed back as written feedback, it lifted mean hardware utilisation from about 4% to about 30%, with individual kernels reaching 70%.

Flip the score and it becomes an attack. Asked to find instruction text that makes a model worse while still stating the task correctly, GEPA discovered that padding a maths prompt with irrelevant true trivia — about honey, rivers, dolphins — collided with a strict output-format rule and caused the model to emit the format placeholder literally instead of an answer.

Where it falls short

  • Maths is the exception. On AIME, retraining won clearly — 38.0 against GEPA's 32.0. Better instructions cannot install a reasoning skill the model does not have.
  • Merge is unreliable. It added points on one model and removed them on the other, using identical settings. When to trigger it is unresolved.
  • The attempt count flatters slightly. Most of GEPA's budget goes on re-scoring survivors for bookkeeping, not on learning. That is honest accounting, but it means the headline ratio would look very different under a different validation scheme — the authors say as much.
  • It needs something to read. The method leans on graders that produce text. Where the only signal is a bare number, the central advantage thins out.
  • Prompt optimisation and retraining are not rivals. Nothing here prevents doing both, and the paper does not test the combination.

The one-line version

Failure already explains itself in English. Reinforcement learning throws that explanation away and reconstructs it, slowly, from scores. GEPA reads it.
