Recursa: an AI system that teaches itself a new field

Recursa makes an AI system better at an unfamiliar field by letting the model write, test, and keep its own notes, with no human expert, no stronger teacher model, and no retraining.

A visual guide to “Recursa: Toward Recursively Self-Improving Foundation Model Systems” by Erkang Zhu, Yin Lin, Xiaorong Shi, Xuanrui Lin and Jingren Zhou of Alibaba, November 2026.

AI models learn most of what they know once, during training, and are then sent into a world that keeps moving. Put one to work at a law firm and it meets client names, documents and turns of phrase it has never seen. The usual remedy is more training, but new fields rarely come with stacks of expert-written examples to train on. This paper tries something else. It leaves the model untouched and gives the system a notebook (the paper calls it a knowledge repository). The system practices, notices what went wrong, writes down a better way of working, checks that the advice really helps, and keeps it. On legal research tasks it has never seen, that’s enough to take one model from 30 fully correct answers out of 100 to 55.

Start with one question

Here is a task from the paper’s test set. A lawyer asks: of all the firm’s merger agreements, which is the most recent signed one that contains a no-shop clause? (A no-shop clause stops a seller from looking for a better offer while a deal is pending.)

The catch is that contracts don’t label things consistently. The same idea might sit under a heading like “Exclusivity” or “Non-solicitation,” and a document can mention the idea without being the right answer. Here is the same model, before and after Recursa had a chance to learn.

Same model, same search tools. The learned version didn’t simply search harder. It carried a rule for recognizing the clause by what it does, a rule the system wrote for itself while practicing on other tasks.

Two different ways to fail

When a model walks into an unfamiliar field, it can come up short in two distinct ways. The paper calls them the knowledge gap and the behavior gap. Think of the field as a board of shaped slots, and what the model knows as pieces.

The knowledge gap is about facts and vocabulary: the model simply doesn’t know what’s in this firm’s files or what its terms mean. The behavior gap is subtler. The model may know the relevant facts and still use them badly, like insisting that a clause must be titled “No-Shop” to count.

Earlier methods tend to work on one gap or the other. Building skills from documents helps with knowledge; learning from task feedback helps with behavior. Some systems reflect on a failed attempt and then retry that same task. Recursa does both kinds of learning, in two phases, and carries each lesson forward to other tasks.

Only the notes change

A modern AI agent is more than a model. Around the model sits a harness: the program that hands it the task, lets it use tools like search, and collects its answer. Recursa adds one more part, the knowledge repository, and that is the only thing that learns. Tap a section to see what goes in it.

Because the repository is plain text, improving the system doesn’t need new training data, a fine-tuning run, or a stronger model to rewrite anything. The model reads its own notes and writes better ones.

Phase one: read the library, then practice

Before the system can learn from mistakes, it needs a first draft of its notes. The paper calls this bootstrapping, and it mostly goes after the knowledge gap. Step through it.

Phase two: learn from each mistake

Now the real learning begins, and it goes after the behavior gap. Copies of the model, called solvers, attempt training tasks. A learner, which is the very same model in a different role, studies how they did and edits the notes. Every round goes around this loop.

The last step deserves a closer look, because it’s what keeps the system from talking itself into bad habits. With no stronger teacher to say what’s right, the only check is evidence: does the edit fix what it was meant to fix, and does it break anything that used to work? Try two candidate edits.

Many learners, one set of notes

A single learner working through 150 tasks in a row would be slow, and its notes could drift toward whatever it saw last. So Recursa divides the work. A parent learner hands groups of tasks to several child learners, then combines what they found.

This is where the word recursive earns its place. The model isn’t only solving tasks. It diagnoses its own failures, rewrites its own guidance, merges its own lessons, and decides how the next round of learning should go. No human expert writes the notes, and no stronger model rewrites them.

That’s hundreds of novels’ worth of reading. Useful knowledge is scattered across files, the same idea goes by different names, and whether an answer is right depends on scope and context, not on finding a matching phrase.

The benchmark has 250 tasks with long written answers. The authors split them into 150 to learn from and 100 held back for the final test, arranged so both halves are about equally hard.

Each task comes with its own checklist of rubric criteria, and a separate, larger model, Qwen3.8-Max, marks each criterion as met or not. The paper reports two scores, and the difference between them matters. Tap the criteria in this made-up example.

The headline number in the paper is the strict one, the all-pass rate: the share of tasks where every single criterion is met.

What happened

Here are the all-pass rates on the 100 held-back tasks. The model is the same in every bar; only the notes differ.

With DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, the system goes from fully solving 30 of 100 unseen tasks to 55. With Qwen3.8-Flash, it climbs from 35 to 46.

The middle bar is the most telling comparison. The single learner started from the same bootstrapped notes, used the same model, and saw the same 150 training tasks, but worked through them alone. It reached 43%. Splitting the work, revising separately, then merging and reflecting reached 55%. Qwen3.8-Flash showed the same pattern, and its single learner even ended up scoring below its starting point on its own training tasks. Editing notes over and over doesn’t automatically make them better.

Reading the result carefully

The gains are on unseen tasks from the same benchmark, not on a different field. The evidence comes from one legal benchmark and two models.

A larger model, Qwen3.8-Max, still grades the rubrics. The authors also found 46 criteria across 30 tasks that contradicted the task wording or the source files, including criteria in 13 test tasks, and they report scores on the corrected rubrics. Their appendix shows the same overall pattern with the original rubrics.

Finally, the many-learner setup bundles several ideas together: splitting the tasks, separate edits, merging, and reflection. The comparison shows the bundle works better than one learner, but it can’t say which ingredient matters most.

The longer road

The title says “toward” for a reason. The authors’ larger goal is a system that improves every part of itself as it meets new fields: the model, the notes, the harness, and the learning process too. This paper builds one layer of that.

Even so, the result points at something practical. When a new field arrives without expert examples or a stronger model to learn from, a system can get meaningfully better by writing, testing and keeping its own notes, without touching a single weight.
