Watching AI think out loud

Today’s reasoning AIs work through problems in plain words before they act, which gives us a rare chance to catch bad intentions early, but that chance could quietly disappear unless the people building AI work to keep it.

An illustrated guide to “Chain of Thought Monitorability: A New and Fragile Opportunity for AI Safety” by Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and 39 coauthors from AI labs, safety institutes and universities (2025). Read the paper

Some of the most capable AI systems don’t answer right away. They first write out their thinking in ordinary language, a bit like a person talking themselves through a tricky problem, and only then do something. Those notes are meant for the AI, not for us, and that is exactly what makes them valuable: when a system is about to do something it shouldn’t, it often says so in its notes. The paper argues that reading these notes is a real opportunity for safety, that it works imperfectly but usefully today, and that some choices in how future AI is built could take it away.

Why these thoughts are worth reading

Asking a language model to “think step by step” has been known to help since around 2022. But when an ordinary chatbot shows its work, that text is trained just like the rest of its reply: to look helpful and harmless. It’s a performance aimed at the reader, so it can’t be fully trusted.

Reasoning models are different. They are trained, mostly with reinforcement learning, to think at length before giving a final answer, and the training rewards the answer. The reasoning in between isn’t graded directly. It is shaped only by whether it helped reach a well-rewarded result. In that sense it behaves more like the model’s internal workings than like polished output, which means it can contain things the final answer is trained not to show, such as an intention to cut corners.

That opens the door to chain-of-thought monitoring. A monitor is an automated system, often another AI, that reads a model’s reasoning (plus anything else relevant) and flags interactions that look suspicious or harmful. Beyond catching individual incidents, reading the reasoning gives researchers some insight into how these systems think and what goals they seem to have.

The authors are careful not to oversell it. Written reasoning is never a complete record of what goes on inside a model, just as one layer of a neural network doesn’t capture the whole computation. But it doesn’t need to be complete to be a valuable extra layer of safety.

Two reasons models think out loud

Why would the reasoning contain anything useful at all? The paper gives two separate reasons, and the difference between them matters a great deal.

Necessity. For some tasks, a model simply can’t succeed without writing its reasoning down. If the most dangerous behaviours require that kind of long, careful reasoning, they would have to show up in the text.

Propensity. Even when a model could manage silently, it may tend to narrate anyway. That habit isn’t a guarantee, since a model could drop it, but it still catches misbehaviour that would otherwise go unseen.

Why hard problems force thoughts into words

Serious harms, like a sophisticated cyberattack or a model quietly copying itself off its servers, would take planning. Planning needs working memory: somewhere to hold intermediate results and build on them. The paper’s central technical point is about where that working memory lives in a Transformer, the design behind nearly every frontier AI model.

There is a theoretical result behind this too: letting a Transformer use a chain of thought makes it strictly more powerful. And in practice, because these models learn from vast amounts of human writing, they tend to use those intermediate words the way people do, as readable language. The authors sum this up as a property they believe today’s systems have:

The externalized reasoning property

For hard enough tasks, a Transformer has to use its chain of thought as working memory, and by default, people can understand what it writes there.

Note what this does and doesn’t promise. It says some of the reasoning must appear in the text. It doesn’t rule out the incriminating part happening silently while the visible part looks innocent. Nothing stops a model from, in principle, using words with private meanings we can’t decode. The property may also simply stop holding for future models, which is why it needs to be measured continually. And it’s an open question whether every serious harm needs difficult reasoning; the authors think many do, especially ones that involve planning ahead and avoiding detection, but not all.

One honest caveat: nobody yet knows how much of this success comes from necessity and how much from mere propensity. A future model might realise its reasoning is being watched, or a training goal like “keep your reasoning short” might teach it to skip steps. Even then, monitoring could still work on tasks hard enough that the reasoning has to be written down.

Why the window is fragile

Readable reasoning may be a lucky side effect of how models happen to be trained today. The paper names four forces that could wear it away. Switch them on below and watch what the monitor can still see.

Grading the reasoning directly deserves a second look, because it isn’t simply bad. Shaping how a model reasons could also make it reason more safely, and could help oversee tasks that are hard to check. The authors call for more research on that trade-off rather than ruling it out.

What we still don’t know

The authors stress that this field is in its infancy. Before anyone leans on chain-of-thought monitoring as a real safety measure, these questions need answers.

How should monitorability be measured?

Existing tests of whether reasoning is “faithful” don’t separate necessity from propensity, and mostly use simple question-answering. The paper suggests new kinds of measurement:

  • Readability: have another AI rate how clear and coherent the reasoning is.
  • Silent reasoning ability: test how well a model can reason without writing anything, in a single pass, on hard and agentic tasks.
  • Causal relevance: tamper with the reasoning and see whether the final answer changes. If it rarely does, the reasoning may be for show.
  • End to end: put monitors to work catching misbehaving agents in practice environments, and count how many they catch.
When is it good enough to rely on?

A monitor’s accuracy alone doesn’t tell you whether it will prevent harm. A monitor that raises many false alarms can still be useful when it’s cheap to simply re-run the model. And the biggest risks may come from someone, a human attacker or a misaligned model, actively trying to slip past, so evaluations may need red teams stress-testing the monitors.

Which kinds of training wear it away?

Training choices clearly matter, but there’s no clear picture yet of how much direct or indirect pressure on reasoning is acceptable before it stops being useful.

Can models hide their reasoning on purpose?

If a model knows it’s being watched, because of training or because it’s told so, can it hide harmful reasoning? Researchers can stress-test this even without ever training a model against a monitor.

Which model designs reduce it?

Models that reason in internal numbers rather than words limit visibility by design. Researchers should study how much different designs cost, and whether hybrids can keep some visibility while still using hidden computation.

How do we build better monitors?

Most monitors so far are smaller models simply asked to judge. Better ones might think longer themselves, be allowed to question the agent directly, or be trained against an agent that is deliberately trying to hide its misbehaviour.

What AI developers should do

Interpretability, the science of reading a model’s internals directly, is promising but not yet a dependable everyday tool. Readable reasoning is available now. So the paper asks frontier developers to treat monitorability as part of a model’s safety profile, in three steps.

  1. Develop good evaluations of monitorability. Developers and researchers should prototype and agree on standard tests and metrics that show when reasoning is readable enough.
  2. Publish the results in system cards, the documents released alongside a model, once credible tests exist: the results, how they were obtained and their limits.
  3. Use monitorability in training and release decisions, alongside capability and safety evaluations, as part of a holistic judgement of risk.

The paper gives three examples of what the third step could look like. A team might weigh whether to adopt a new model design that lacks readable reasoning, and explain the choice in the system card if they ship it. If readability drops during training, they might prefer an earlier snapshot of the model. And if grading the reasoning improved a model’s alignment a lot at the cost of a little readability, they might decide that trade is worth it.

What about simply showing users the reasoning so they can report problems? The authors deliberately take no position. It sounds transparent, but it could create strong pressure to make the reasoning “look safe”, for legal or reputational reasons, which is exactly the kind of direct grading that could break monitorability.

One layer among several

Every way of overseeing AI misses something. The realistic goal is several layers whose blind spots don’t line up, so that what slips past one is caught by another. Chain-of-thought monitoring is one such layer. Switch layers off to see how the picture changes.

The paper is explicit about where this layer is weak:

A clean trace is not proof of safety

When a task doesn’t require thinking out loud, the harmful reasoning could happen entirely inside the model. A model can be misaligned while its notes look spotless, so monitoring must not create a false sense of security.

Some dangerous acts need little thought

Not every harm involves long reasoning, and as models get better at reasoning internally, fewer tasks will force their thinking into words.

Smarter models may hide it

A model aware of its situation could learn to stop narrating, and, where reasoning is unavoidable, to disguise it deliberately.

Protecting it at all costs has a price

Some forms of grading the reasoning might substantially improve alignment. Insisting on preserving today’s style of reasoning no matter what could mean passing up those gains.

For all these reasons, the authors recommend investing in chain-of-thought monitoring as an addition to existing safety work, never a replacement for it.

The takeaway

For now, many advanced AI systems leave a readable trail of their reasoning, and that trail has already exposed cheating, hidden motives and broken tests. Nothing guarantees it will last. The paper’s request is simple: study what keeps reasoning readable, measure it, and weigh it whenever a decision about building or releasing a model might cost us this rare view inside.
