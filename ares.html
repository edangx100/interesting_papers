How ARES grades an AI that looks things up

ARES trains three small judges on practice questions it writes itself, then uses a few hundred human-checked examples to correct their mistakes and attach honest error bars to every score.

A plain-language guide to “ARES: An Automated Evaluation Framework for Retrieval-Augmented Generation Systems” by Jon Saad-Falcon, Omar Khattab, Christopher Potts and Matei Zaharia (Stanford University and Databricks). Read the paper.

Many AI assistants don’t answer from memory alone. They first search a library of documents, pick a passage that seems useful, and then write an answer based on it. Building one of these means making dozens of choices, and the only reliable way to know which choices work has been to have people read and grade hundreds of answers for every version you try. That is slow and expensive. This paper describes a way to do most of that grading automatically, while still being able to say how much you should trust the result.

First, what is being graded

The kind of system in question is called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. It has two working parts: a retriever that searches a collection of documents, and a language model that reads what was found and writes the reply.

Three questions every answer has to pass

ARES doesn’t hand out a single “quality” score. It splits the job into three yes-or-no checks, each about the link between two of the three pieces: the question, the passage, and the answer.

Context relevance. Did the retriever find a passage that actually helps answer this question?

Answer faithfulness. Does the answer stick to what the passage says, or does it invent or stretch things?

Answer relevance. Does the answer respond to what was asked?

Splitting it up is useful because the three can fail independently. A system can fetch the perfect passage and still write a reply that wanders off topic. Knowing which link broke tells you whether to fix the search or the writing.

Why this is hard to measure

Before ARES, teams had two broad options. They could have people label test answers by hand, which is trustworthy but costly and often needs domain experts. Or they could ask a large language model to do the grading using a fixed, hand-written prompt. That is cheap, and it is what an open-source tool called RAGAS does, but the prompt is the same whatever your documents are about, and nothing tells you how far off its grades might be.

ARES aims for the corner neither option reaches: little human effort and a stated margin of error.

How ARES works, in three stages

You give ARES three things: the documents your system searches, about 150 or more examples that a person has labelled as good or bad on the three checks, and at least five sample question-and-answer pairs from your domain. It then runs three stages in order.

1It writes its own practice exam

To train a judge you need lots of examples of good and bad question–passage–answer combinations. Nobody wants to write those by hand, so ARES has a language model (the paper uses FLAN-T5 XXL) read each passage in your collection and write a question that the passage answers, plus an answer to it.

Some of those generated questions are poor. ARES weeds them out with a neat round-trip test: it runs the question through a search and keeps it only if the passage it came from comes back as the top result. If a question can’t find its own source, it is probably too vague.

A judge also needs to see what wrong looks like, so ARES manufactures bad examples in two strengths, in equal numbers.

2It trains three small, specialised judges

With that practice data, ARES fine-tunes three separate classifiers, one per check. Each reads the question, passage and answer joined together and outputs a single verdict: pass or fail on its one check.

The judges are deliberately small. The paper uses DeBERTa-v3-Large, a model of about 304 million parameters, which runs on your own hardware without calling a paid API. Being trained on your documents is the whole point: a judge tuned to your legal contracts or product manuals has seen what good and bad look like there, not just in general.

3It corrects the judges using real human labels

Here is the clever part. The judges were trained on made-up data, so they will be wrong in some consistent way. Perhaps they are a little too generous, or too strict. If you simply averaged their verdicts, that bias would flow straight into your scores.

ARES uses a recent statistical method called prediction-powered inference (PPI). The idea is simple even if the maths isn’t:

Let the judge grade thousands of unlabelled answers, which is cheap. Then run the same judge on the small set that humans labelled and see how often, and in which direction, it disagrees with people. That tells you the judge’s typical error, which you subtract from the big estimate. Because you know how noisy the correction itself is, you also get a range that should contain the true score 95% of the time.

Why not just use the human labels on their own? You could, but a few hundred labels give a wide, blurry range. PPI borrows strength from the thousands of cheap judge verdicts, so the same human effort buys a much tighter range. Try it:

Finally, ARES ranks systems by the midpoint of each range. And because the judges are trained once per domain and the same human labels serve for every system, testing a tenth configuration costs no more human effort than testing the first.

How the authors checked that it works

To grade a grader, you need systems whose true quality you already know. The authors built fake RAG systems out of six standard question-answering datasets (such as Natural Questions, HotpotQA and FEVER) by mixing correct and incorrect examples in controlled proportions. For each dataset they made nine systems whose true pass rates run from 70% to 90% in steps of 2.5 points.

They measured success with Kendall’s tau, a score for how well one ranking matches another. It looks at every pair of systems and asks whether the evaluator put them in the right order. With nine systems there are 36 pairs, which makes the numbers easy to read: a tau of 1.0 means every pair is right, 0.94 means one pair is swapped, 0.89 means two, and 0.83 means three. The authors treat anything above 0.9 as a success.

What they found

The judges are much more accurate than a generic prompt

On individual examples, ARES’s trained judges got the context-relevance verdict right about 83% of the time on average, against 23% for RAGAS. RAGAS did better on answer relevance, but ARES still led by about 14 points. A GPT-3.5 judge given a few examples landed in between.

ARES ranks systems in the right order more often

What matters most is whether the final ranking is right. ARES had the highest average tau of every method tried, clearing the 0.9 bar on both checks. The same judges without the PPI correction scored lower on average and never higher on any dataset, so the human-label correction is doing real work.

It needs far fewer human labels

The most striking comparison is with plain hand-labelling. Labelling 150 answers from each of the nine systems takes 1,350 labels. ARES used 300 labels in total and still ranked the systems better, 0.08 higher in tau on average. That is 78% less human work.

But there is a floor

How few labels can you get away with? The authors tried sets from 25 to 400. Below about 150, ARES could no longer reliably tell the close-together systems apart, especially on context relevance. More labels kept helping above that.

It spots made-up answers in real systems

The fake systems couldn’t test faithfulness, so the authors turned to a benchmark called AIS, where people had marked real generated answers as supported by their source or not. Using 200 human labels, ARES estimated the share of faithful answers to within 2.5 points of the truth on both datasets.

They also ranked ten real RAG systems, built from three retrievers (BM25, OpenAI embeddings and ColBERTv2) and three writers (MPT-7B-Instruct, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4), plus Facebook’s original RAG model. ARES averaged a tau of 0.91 on context relevance and 0.97 on answer relevance, again ahead of RAGAS, and its 95% ranges really did contain the true score more than 95% of the time. For the record, ColBERTv2 was the best retriever and GPT-4 the best writer.

Where it stops working

A judge trained on one dataset held up well on another when the questions changed style, when the documents changed, or both, staying near the 0.9 line. Even when a judge’s own accuracy dropped away from home, the PPI correction mostly rescued the ranking.

Bigger leaps broke it. A judge trained on English questions about Wikipedia fell apart on other languages, on searching code, and on a task that pulls out named entities rather than answering questions.

The authors also note the practical costs. The human labels have to come from someone who understands the domain, which is easy for general knowledge but harder for law, medicine or finance. The models need a GPU with roughly 32 GB of memory, and generating data and training take several hours. And every test was in English.

What to take away

Grading a RAG system works better as three separate questions than as one: is the passage useful, is the answer grounded in it, and does the answer address the question.

A small judge trained on your own documents beats a big general model following a generic prompt, and you can build its training data automatically.

A few hundred human labels are still essential, but their job changes: rather than doing the grading, they check and correct the automatic graders, and give you a margin of error you can trust.
