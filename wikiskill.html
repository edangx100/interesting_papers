What the paper is about

A "skill" here is just a folder of written instructions that an AI agent reads before it starts working — the same way a new hire reads a how-to doc. Recent systems let the agent write those instructions itself: it attempts a batch of tasks, looks at where it went wrong, rewrites the doc, and tries again.

The catch is that everything the agent figured out along the way — why an attempt failed, which fix was already tried and didn't work — lives only in the scratch reasoning of one round. Next round it starts cold. This paper adds a second file that is never thrown away: a wiki. The agent keeps writing observations into it, and every future rewrite of the instructions gets to read the whole thing. The instructions can be undone; the wiki cannot.

That one change is the entire idea. The authors report that it beats the three leading alternatives on every model they tried.

The setup

Three folders, four jobs

The agent's workspace is split into three directories that are deliberately not allowed to blur into each other.

FolderWhat lives thereChanges how
raw/ Complete transcripts of every attempt — reasoning, tool calls, outputs, final answers. append only
wiki/ Short markdown pages, one per recurring failure or working tactic, plus a log of every past proposal and whether it was kept. grows, never reverts
skills/ The instructions the agent actually reads while working. Each has a SKILL.md and a note pointing back to the wiki pages that motivated it. can be rolled back

Four separate agents take turns on those folders, once per round:

  1. Inference Agent

    Attempts the training tasks with the current instructions pasted into its prompt. It is deliberately blocked from reading the wiki — more on why below. Its transcripts land in raw/.

  2. Wiki Maintainer

    Reads a sample of the successes and failures, diagnoses root causes, and edits the wiki: new pages for new problems, patches to existing pages when fresh evidence shows up.

  3. Skill Proposer

    Starts from the wiki index and the running record of what was accepted or rejected before, then pulls up specific pages and transcripts on demand. It proposes exactly one change per round — a new skill, or a patch to one existing skill.

  4. Gate

    Runs the proposed instructions against a held-out validation set. Better score, the change sticks. Worse or equal, the instructions revert. Either way, the outcome and the exact diff get appended to the wiki.

The core idea

Failed attempts still teach something

Most rounds end in rejection. In the authors' own runs, roughly three of every eight proposals survive the gate. Under the older methods, the seven-eighths that fail leave nothing behind — the instructions snap back and the next round has no idea that fix was already tried.

WikiSkill records the rejected diff and the reason it failed. So the proposer stops re-proposing dead ends, and starts seeing which errors keep coming back across rounds rather than just the ones in front of it.

Results

Three findings worth the space

Bigger models get more out of it, not less

Within one model family the gain over no skills grows with size: +12.3 points at 4B, +17.5 at 9B, +23.9 at 27B. Written instructions aren't a crutch for weak models — stronger models both write better ones and follow them more reliably. And the 9B model with evolved skills (47.4) beats the 27B model without them (39.4).

The instructions travel between models

Skills written by one model often work better in a different one, sometimes better than that model's own. On the household simulator, the 9B model scored 63.4 with skills it wrote itself and 70.2 with skills written by the 27B model. Even upward transfer works: 4B-written maths skills lifted Gemma-4-31B from 33.9 to 73.1. Discovering a good procedure and executing one are apparently separate abilities.

…until they don't

Transfer fails when a skill encodes a workaround for one model's quirks rather than a general procedure. Spreadsheet skills written by the 4B model — one-line Python commands, string-conversion rules that stop it crashing — dropped Gemini-3.5-Flash from 50.5 to 18.1 by blocking it from writing the full scripts it would normally use.

The check that matters

Is it really the wiki?

Two things fall out of this. Giving the proposer the wiki is worth about 15 points — that is the paper's central claim, and it holds. Less obviously, giving the wiki to the inference agent makes things slightly worse (63.7 down to 60.9). The authors' explanation: if the agent can crib answers from the wiki while attempting tasks, its transcripts stop revealing what the instructions were actually missing. The training runs have to be honest about the agent's weaknesses to be useful.

Read with

What this doesn't show

  • Every skill is pasted into the prompt. The authors do this on purpose, to measure the quality of the writing rather than an agent's ability to find the right file. Once a library has hundreds of skills, retrieval becomes its own problem, untouched here.
  • The wiki only grows. There is no pruning. Over long runs it will outgrow whatever context the maintainer has to read it with, and nobody has tested where that ceiling is.
  • The gate is strict. A proposal that leaves the score unchanged is rejected, even if it would have set up a gain two rounds later. That's inherited from earlier work for comparability, and the authors flag it as worth relaxing.
  • Nothing here runs for hours. The longest tasks are multi-step tool use, not the hundreds-of-actions horizons where knowing what you learned an hour ago matters most.
  • Benchmarks are still benchmarks. Gains of forty points on spreadsheet manipulation are gains on one curated dataset, with a scorer that has its own idea of what counts as correct.

The takeaway

Why it's interesting

The result is less about a framework and more about a distinction. Everything an agent learns tends to get compressed straight into instructions, because instructions are what the agent reads. This paper says: don't. Keep the evidence separate from the procedure, let the evidence build up, and rewrite the procedure from the accumulated evidence each time.

That is roughly how a team wiki works next to a runbook, and it's a fairly cheap thing to add to any agent setup that already keeps logs.
