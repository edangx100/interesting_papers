AI agents push humans out of the loop

Telling a person to supervise an AI agent doesn’t make the system safe, because supervising is the very job that wears the supervisor down.

An explainer of the position paper AI Agents Push Humans Out of the Loop by Margaret Mitchell and Avijit Ghosh (Hugging Face) and Samir Passi (Data & Society). Posted to arXiv in August 2026, revised September 2026. Read the original. Everything below is a summary in my own words.

The promise: someone is still watching

Whenever software is trusted with something that matters — a diagnosis, a payment, a change to a company’s files — the standard reassurance is that a person stays in charge of it. Regulators write this into law. Companies write it into their safety documents. Products write it into the little line under the text box asking you to double-check the answer.

The three authors argue that this reassurance is doing far more work than it can bear. Their claim is not that supervision is a bad idea. It is that the way AI agents are currently built and rolled out makes real supervision harder over time, and quietly erodes the human abilities that supervision depends on. A person clicking “approve” is not the same thing as a person exercising judgement.

What the watcher is actually being asked to do

Supervision used to mean checking a result. An older system produced a score or a prediction, and a person decided whether it looked right. That is a small, bounded task.

A chatbot made it bigger: now there is a page of fluent text to verify, produced faster than anyone can read it carefully, with mistakes that look exactly like the correct parts. An agent makes it bigger again. It plans, calls tools, writes files, sends things, changes things, and then reacts to whatever comes back — often across many steps that were never specified in advance.

To supervise that well, a person would have to hold the whole plan in their head while it is running, judge each step as a domain expert, imagine the consequences of steps that haven’t happened yet, and stay alert through hundreds of routine approvals so they catch the one that matters. That is a working-memory task no interface currently supports.

Watching is the thing that wears the watcher down

This is the part of the paper that does the real work. It draws on forty years of research into automation, going back to Lisanne Bainbridge’s observation in 1983 that the better an automated system gets, the worse its human operator becomes at the very moments the operator is needed. She called it the irony of automation.

The evidence the authors gather is uncomfortable:

  • Skills fade when they are not used. A study of endoscopists found their unassisted detection rates dropped after regular exposure to an AI aid.
  • Effort drops too. In an EEG study of people writing essays with a language model, brain connectivity was measurably lower than in people writing unaided.
  • People substitute cheap signals for real checking. Developers in one study treated an agent’s stated plan as a reliable account of what it would actually do, and treated passing tests as proof the code was correct.
  • Approval fatigue sets in. After enough prompts, clicking through stops being a decision and becomes a reflex.
  • Newcomers may never build the judgement in the first place. You cannot check work you never learned to do.
  • Experts over-rely too, and most sharply just outside their own specialty — an experienced developer working in an unfamiliar language, for instance.

None of this requires anyone to be careless. It is what sustained attention to a fluent, usually-correct system does to ordinary people.

“Oversight degrades the overseer.”

The failure feeds back into the system

Here the argument turns from a human problem into a machine-learning one. Models are shaped by human feedback, and deployed agents are often judged by approval rates, completion rates and satisfaction scores.

An alert reviewer withholds approval when something looks thin. A tired one approves quickly and rates the interaction well. If those approvals are counted as evidence that the system worked, then the system is being rewarded for producing whatever a tired reviewer waves through: confident summaries, fewer interruptions, plans that are easy to skim.

Nobody has to intend this. It happens whenever the thing being measured — the person’s approval — drifts away from the thing that was meant — a correct action that was actually scrutinised.

What they propose instead

The proposal is not to slow agents down for its own sake. It is to treat the supervisor’s attention and skill as a design constraint with the same standing as agent capability — and to accept that no interface fix alone can carry it. Their inventory splits across who can act and what is being protected.

Built into the product, by developers
Set up around the work, by the organisations deploying it
Protecting attention in the moment

Friction placed on purpose

Ask for the person’s own call before showing the agent’s. Let them choose whether to see the output at all. Ask what evidence would change their mind, at the moments that matter. Require an explicit go-ahead before a consequential path.

Approval designed as a scarce resource

Agree in advance what needs no sign-off. Batch related work into one reviewable diff rather than a stream of prompts. Let machines pre-check the things that need no judgement.

Measuring whether review is real

Watch for review time falling while approvals hold steady, disagreement rates drying up, and reviewers who stop asking questions as stakes rise.

Approval and monitoring, again

The same signals, read by the organisation: a second reviewer for consequential actions, and attention paid to whether review is holding up across a team.

Workload and scheduling

Enforced breaks, and rotations between tasks — including between assisted and unassisted versions of the same task.

Protecting skill over time

Canaries

Slip tasks with known answers into the normal flow. If they are waved through, judgement has slipped, and that can trigger a break or retraining. The same trick works on the agent: design tasks where the right move is to admit it cannot do the job.

Training and exercises

Do the underlying work unaided sometimes, so the expertise survives. Teach people what errors to expect and how to notice their own attention flagging.

Role design

Give oversight to someone with the expertise to do it, who does not personally benefit from the approval, and whose targets do not punish them for slowing down.

Audits

Revisit past decisions after the fact, and check the agent’s account of what it did against the raw logs.

The authors are careful to call this a map rather than a taxonomy — a way of finding the gaps, not a finished answer.

Objections they take seriously

People adapt. We learned to calibrate our trust in search results and autocomplete; we’ll learn this too.

Their reply: a search engine hands back documents you evaluate at your own pace. An agent takes actions you are expected to authorise while they are happening. And agent capability is moving faster than the research on how people cope with it — adaptation is not arriving on schedule.

This is a tooling problem. Better traces, better explanations, better logs.

Their reply: partly agreed, and some of their own proposals are exactly that. But explanations rely on the alertness that heavy agent use erodes, and can anchor a reviewer to a wrong conclusion. No interface change fixes a six-hour approval session or a reviewer whose targets reward speed.

Alignment will make this moot. Train on human preferences and oversight becomes unnecessary.

Their reply: this assumes user preference is a good proxy for oversight quality, and the evidence suggests it isn’t — people prefer fluent, agreeable, confident output, which is precisely what reduces scrutiny. Preference training can be part of the answer only if approval is audited against something independent.

The point to retain

If a system is only safe because a person is watching, then that person’s attention, skill, and willingness to disagree are critical supports of the system — and they are currently the supports nobody is maintaining.
