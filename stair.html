STAIR: ask the table of contents

Give a language model the book's contents page along with the question, and it names the one section that holds the answer — right 82.6% of the time on the first try, and almost never inventing a section that doesn't exist.

Kumar, Pulivarthi, Kumar, Sen, Bhat and Joshi, IBM. arXiv:2609.03874, September 2026.

Set for long reading: warm-grey paper instead of white, lines under 70 characters, and no motion unless you ask for it.

1

The idea

When you want to know something from a thick book, you don't read it. You flip to the contents page, run your eye down the chapter names, pick the one that sounds right, then narrow to a section inside it. You find the answer without having read a word of the body text. Software search does almost the opposite: it slices the book into equal-sized blocks of text and compares your question against every block. The contents page — the author's own map of what is where — gets thrown away before the search begins. STAIR is what happens when you hand that map back.

The map is a tree. Chapters contain sections, sections contain subsections, and at the bottom are the entries with no children beneath them. The paper calls those bottom entries leaf nodes, and they are what STAIR returns: not a paragraph, not a page range, but the name of one leaf. Ask about plurality voting and it answers “4.2.3 Electoral Systems & Political Parties.” The text under that heading is where you read next.

Two things make this more than a nice metaphor. First, a leaf section is a unit an author already decided was one topic, so its edges fall in sensible places — unlike a 500-word window, which starts and stops wherever the counter runs out. Second, the set of valid answers is small and known in advance: a book has a few hundred leaves, and nothing else counts as an answer. That turns a fuzzy matching problem into something closer to multiple choice.

1.1

What chunking loses

There is a second reason not to just paste the whole book into the model's context and let it read. Long-context models degrade in a well-documented way: they attend to the beginning and the end of a long input and lose material buried in the middle. Feeding a 1,300-page textbook to a model per question is both expensive and unreliable, which is why precise retrieval still matters even as context windows grow.

2

A benchmark had to be built first

Nobody could test this idea, because no existing dataset had what it needed: long documents, a clean structural map, and questions labelled with the exact section that answers them. Summarisation benchmarks have no gold passage. Question-answering benchmarks give the answer text but not its location. The closest fit, LocoV1, has gold passages but no contents page.

So the authors built SearchTome from open textbooks: three books each from education, finance, law, medicine, natural sciences and social sciences. They parsed each PDF, pulled out the contents tree, matched every entry to its body text, then had Mixtral 8×7B write questions about each paragraph — splitting them into training, tuning and test sets.

3

How STAIR is built

STAIR fine-tunes Mistral 7B Instruct v0.2 with a LoRA adapter — a small set of added weights, rank 16, rather than retraining the whole model. Training stops early based on top-1 accuracy on the held-out development questions. The input can run to 14,000 tokens, because the entire contents page rides along with every question.

3.1

What the model actually sees

This is the whole trick, and it is almost embarrassingly plain. The comparison system, DSI, is trained on question-and-answer pairs alone: it has to infer the book's structure indirectly, from thousands of examples, and store it in its weights. STAIR gets handed the structure as part of every prompt.

### Book: Teaching in a Digital Age Pick the best section from the table of contents below which can answer the user query below. Only generate the section name, do not generate any explanation! ### Table of Contents 1 Chapter 1: Fundamental Change in Education ⋯ leaf 12.7 Step five: master the technology leaf 12.8 Step six: set appropriate learning goals leaf 12.9 Step seven: design course structure and learning ⋯ leaf 13.7 Building the future ### Query: What is the suggested time allocation for students studying a course or program? ### Section: leaf → 12.9 Step seven: design course structure and learning

Note the word “leaf” marking which entries are answerable. The contents page is identical for every question about that book, so the model sees the same map thousands of times during training and learns to navigate it, while also absorbing the book's content into its weights as it goes.

Two results in that table are worth pausing on. An untuned Mistral, handed the same contents page and asked the same question, gets 13.8% — the structure alone is useless without training on the corpus. And BM25, a keyword-matching method from the 1990s, beats a frontier model used off the shelf. Neither the knowledge nor the task can be skipped.

4.1

It stops inventing sections

A retriever that returns a heading which doesn't exist in the book is worse than useless — the answer looks plausible and leads nowhere. The paper counts these directly: any output that isn't a real leaf is a hallucination. Untuned Mistral produces them constantly, roughly a quarter of its mistakes. Fine-tuned DSI cuts that to 3.25% of all its predictions. STAIR gets it to 0.05%.

4.2

The gap is widest where the data is thinnest

This is the most interesting finding in the paper, and it explains the mechanism. Some sections of a book attract many training questions; others attract only a handful. DSI has to memorise every section name from those examples, so the rarely-taught ones are the ones it forgets and misses. STAIR never has to remember them — they are right there in the prompt. As the number of training examples per section rises, DSI catches up. Where examples are scarce, STAIR is far ahead.

5

One query, five systems

From The Whole Child: Development in the Early Years. The question is “How do preschoolers react when caregivers and teachers belittle their autonomous actions?” The right answer is section 2.2.3, Initiative vs. Guilt (Preschool Years).

Mistral Listed three sections at once, including the correct one. Its world knowledge was enough to guess, but nobody taught it that the task is to pick exactly one.
BM25 8.3.2 Moral Development Chosen purely for word overlap with the query. The classic failure of keyword matching.
DPR 2.2.2 Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt (Toddlerhood) Latched onto “autonomous” and landed one stage too early in childhood. Close, but it was never trained on this book.
DSI 2.2.2 Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt (Preschool Years) No such heading exists. It blended the toddler section's title with the preschool label from elsewhere — a fluent, confident invention.
STAIR 2.2.3 Initiative vs. Guilt (Preschool Years)

DSI's answer is the one to study. It is not a random error — it is a better guess than DPR's, showing it had learned that “preschool” matters here. But because it was reciting section names from memory rather than choosing from a list, it produced a heading the book doesn't contain. Having the contents page in front of it would have made that answer impossible.

6

What it can't do yet

STAIR only works on documents that already have a structural map. Textbooks, manuals, regulatory filings and product documentation qualify; a pile of loose emails or scraped web pages does not. The authors name this as their main limitation and want to try generating a contents-page structure for corpora that lack one.

There is also a cost that comes with the design. The model is fine-tuned per corpus, its knowledge baked into the weights, so adding a chapter means retraining rather than re-indexing. And because the entire contents page travels with every question, each query carries thousands of tokens of overhead — fine for a 400-leaf textbook, an open question for an enterprise corpus with millions of entries, which is exactly where the authors want to test next.

The direction they are most interested in is iterative: a retriever that walks down the tree one level at a time, reads what it finds, and decides where to look next — the way a person actually uses a book when the first guess doesn't pan out.
