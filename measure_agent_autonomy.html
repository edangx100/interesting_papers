Measuring AI agent autonomy in practice

People are letting AI agents work on their own for longer, and they supervise them differently as trust grows, while most of what agents actually do stays low-risk and easy to undo.

An illustrated walk through Anthropic’s February 2026 study of millions of real agent interactions. About ten minutes to read.

An AI agent is an AI that can do things, not just talk: run code, edit files, send a message. Lab tests tell us what agents are capable of. This study asks a more down-to-earth question: what happens when real people actually use them? The researchers looked at millions of real interactions, using a privacy-preserving tool that surfaces patterns without anyone reading individual conversations, and measured four things: how long agents work before stopping, how people keep watch, when agents stop to ask for help, and how risky their actions are.

First, what counts as an agent?

There is no agreed definition, so the authors picked one they could actually measure: an agent is an AI system equipped with tools that let it take actions. That turns a fuzzy idea into something you can count. Every time the model reaches out and touches the world, that is one action.

Two windows onto the same world

Anthropic has two very different views of agents in use, and each covers the other’s blind spot.

The public API is how thousands of companies build their own agents on Claude. It is broad, but Anthropic can’t see how those companies’ systems fit together, so it can’t stitch separate requests into sessions. It can only study each action on its own.

Claude Code, Anthropic’s own coding agent, is the opposite. It is a single product, mostly used for software, but every session can be followed from start to finish: how long Claude worked, when it paused, when the person stepped in.

How long does Claude work before it stops?

In Claude Code, a turn is the stretch between Claude starting work and stopping, whether because it finished, asked a question, or got interrupted. Turn length is an imperfect measure of autonomy, since faster models shorten turns and harder tasks lengthen them, but it is the most direct one available.

Most turns are short. The typical turn, the median, lasts about 45 seconds, and that has barely moved in months. So the interesting part is at the far end.

Between October 2025 and January 2026, that one-in-a-thousand turn nearly doubled in length, from under 25 minutes to over 45 minutes.

The shape of the rise matters as much as its size. If longer turns came purely from smarter models, you would expect a staircase: a jump at each model launch. Instead the climb is smooth. That points to other forces too: power users gradually trusting the tool, taking on bigger tasks, and the product itself improving week by week.

Is 45 minutes a lot?

It helps to put these durations on one scale. Because they span seconds to hours, the axis below is logarithmic: each step to the right multiplies time rather than adding to it.

Anthropic’s own internal usage points the same way. From August to December, Claude Code’s success rate on the hardest internal tasks doubled, while the number of times a person had to step in per session fell.

The authors call this a deployment overhang: models can already handle more independence than people give them in practice.

Trust changes how people supervise

Claude Code asks for approval before each action by default. People can switch on full auto-approve and let it run. New users (fewer than 50 sessions) do that in roughly 20% of sessions. By 750 sessions it is over 40%.

You might guess experienced users simply stop paying attention. But they also interrupt more: about 5% of turns for someone around ten sessions in, rising to about 9% for seasoned users. Drag the slider to watch both happen at once.

These two trends only look contradictory. They describe a change of strategy: from a gatekeeper who approves every step, to a monitor who lets work flow and steps in when something looks off.

The public API shows a related pattern. Simple actions, like editing one line of code, involve a human in some way 87% of the time. Complex ones, like hunting for security flaws or writing a compiler, only 67%. Approving every step simply stops being practical when there are hundreds of steps.

Good oversight does not mean approving every action. It means being in a position to step in when it matters.

The agent stops itself, too

People are not the only ones applying the brakes. Claude pauses to ask when it is unsure how to proceed, and on the most complex goals it does so more than twice as often as on the simplest ones. On those hardest goals, Claude stops to ask more than twice as often as people interrupt it.

Why do these stops happen? Claude summarized the reasons across a sample of half a million pauses and half a million interruptions. The two lists are revealing side by side: Claude mostly stops to offer choices, while people mostly step in to supply context Claude lacked.

The authors are careful not to oversell this. Claude may not always stop at the right moment or may ask questions it didn’t need to. Still, a model that recognizes its own uncertainty is a safety feature that works alongside permissions and human review, not instead of them.

What are agents doing, and how risky is it?

Here the study switches to the broad API view: a random sample of almost a million individual tool calls. Claude rated each action on two 1-to-10 scales. Risk: how bad would it be if this went wrong? Autonomy: is the agent following explicit instructions, or acting on its own? Similar actions were then grouped into clusters.

The headline numbers are reassuring. About 80% of actions come from agents with at least one safeguard, such as restricted permissions or required approval. About 73% have a human in the loop somehow. And only a sliver can’t be undone.

Averages hide the edges, though. The chart below plots some of the most extreme clusters. Most activity crowds into the low-risk band at the bottom. The top-right corner, high risk and high autonomy, is sparse, but not empty.

Where are agents working? Mostly in one place. Software engineering accounts for nearly half of all agent tool calls. Everything else, from customer service to finance, is a long tail of small slices.

Software went first partly because it is easy to check: you can run code and see whether it works. In law, medicine or finance, verifying an agent’s output can take as much expertise as producing it, which may slow the growth of trust. As agents spread there, the authors expect the frontier of risk and autonomy to widen.

Autonomy is made by three parties

The central lesson is that autonomy is not a fixed property of a model. You can’t read it off a benchmark. It emerges from how the model behaves, how the person chooses to watch, and how the product is designed.

What the authors suggest

Because this kind of measurement is so new, the recommendations are deliberately modest.

Keep watching after launch

Pre-release testing shows what an agent can do in a controlled setting. Only monitoring after deployment shows how people really use it. One gap to close: finding privacy-preserving ways to link separate API requests into whole agent sessions.

Teach models to know when they don’t know

Training agents to notice their own uncertainty and raise it with a person complements external guardrails. Anthropic trains Claude this way and encourages other developers to do the same.

Design for watching, not just approving

Since experienced users shift toward monitoring, products should give people a clear, trustworthy view of what the agent is doing and simple ways to redirect it mid-task.

Don’t mandate a single interaction pattern yet

Rules requiring a human to approve every action would add friction without clearly adding safety. What matters is whether people are in a position to monitor and intervene, not the specific ritual they use.

What this study can’t see

The data covers only Anthropic’s models, over a short window from late 2025 to early 2026. The richest findings come from Claude Code, which is overwhelmingly used for software and may not generalize. Risk and autonomy scores were assigned by Claude and are best read as comparisons rather than precise measurements, and privacy rules mean humans couldn’t inspect the raw data. Sampling by individual tool call over-represents workflows that take many small steps. And from a single API call, Anthropic can’t always tell a real action from a test, or see human review that happens further downstream.
