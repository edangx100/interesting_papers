Self-improvement that holds up on new work

RRSI lets an AI agent keep rewriting its own setup, but only keeps changes that are small, affordable and not tailored to its practice tasks, so the gains carry over to work it has never seen.

A visual guide to RRSI: Regularized Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses by Peng Xia, Rujun Han, Zifeng Wang and 11 co-authors at Google Cloud AI Research, Stanford, Washington University in St. Louis and UNC-Chapel Hill (2026).

An AI assistant is more than the model underneath it. Around the model sits a layer of instructions, tools and bookkeeping that decides how it goes about a job, and that layer can be rewritten without retraining anything. A recent idea is to let the AI rewrite it by itself: try some practice tasks, look at what went wrong, change something, repeat. The catch is the same one faced by a student who drills last year's exam. The score goes up; the skill doesn't. This paper borrows a very old fix from statistics and applies it to how an agent is allowed to change itself.

The model stays fixed. Everything around it can change.

Think of an AI agent as two parts. There is the model, the trained network that actually reads and writes. In this paper it is frozen: its weights never change. Around it sits the harness: the instructions it gets, the order in which it plans and acts, the tools it can call, the notes it keeps, and what it is shown at each step. The same model can do far better or far worse depending on its harness.

An agent that edits its own harness

Harness design used to be manual: an engineer reads transcripts of failures and tweaks the setup by hand. Recent methods give that job to AI. Each round, the agent attempts a fixed set of practice tasks. One model summarizes what went wrong, another proposes edits, and whichever version scores best becomes the new harness. Then the loop runs again.

Because the system uses its own results to reshape itself, the authors treat this as a practical form of recursive self-improvement, happening at the level of the whole agent rather than its weights.

Why practising on the same tasks goes wrong

The practice set never changes. Every round takes another look at the same tasks, and every look is a fresh chance to fit their quirks instead of learning anything general. Statisticians call this overfitting: a curve bent to pass through every data point, which then misses every new one.

In harness evolution, the authors see overfitting creep in through three coupled behaviours.

The first is memorizing: edits that quietly encode task names, specific values or even answers. The second is chasing noise. An agent's score wobbles from run to run, so if you compare many candidates and crown the highest, you are partly crowning luck, and that luck then becomes a permanent part of the harness. The third is piling on machinery: extra steps, checks and context that nudge the practice score up while making every run longer and more expensive.

The pattern shows up in the numbers. On legal and office tasks, the strongest earlier method raised its practice score by 3.6 points but gained less than one point on benchmarks it hadn't seen. Two others finished below the harness they started from.

The idea: constrain the path, not the destination

An obvious fix would be to limit what the agent may edit, say, prompts only. RRSI does the opposite. Every part of the harness stays editable: prompts, control flow, tools, skills, memory, even sub-agents. What RRSI constrains is how the search moves: how much it may change at once, where it looks, and which results it is willing to believe.

That is the heart of regularization in statistics. When data is limited and noisy, prefer simpler explanations and ask for stronger evidence before accepting complicated ones. RRSI applies seven such rules, split across the two halves of every round: three that shape where the search looks and four that decide what it is allowed to keep.

Three of these rules mirror textbook regularizers. The paper is careful to call them analogies rather than exact equivalents, but the geometric intuition carries over nicely.

Where the search looks

A shrinking edit budget

Early on, a candidate may bundle several edits, three or four depending on the domain, so the search can find ideas that only work together. The allowance then shrinks along a cosine curve to a single edit by the final round. Late changes are small enough that you can tell which one caused an improvement.

bt=⌈ bmin+(bmax−bmin)·½(1+cos(πt/T)) ⌉

A ledger of everything tried

Every candidate is written down: which part it touched, the hypothesis behind it, the exact change, and how score and cost moved. The proposer reads this record each round. Ideas that failed stay on file as evidence against themselves, so the search stops spending rounds rediscovering them.

Exploring when progress stalls

The same record reveals when the search is stuck in a rut, endlessly rewording prompts, for example. If three rounds pass with no progress beyond the noise level, one proposal slot is set aside for a part of the harness that hasn't been touched yet.

What the search is allowed to keep

Screening for leaks

Before any candidate is scored, a separate critic model reads its changes and rejects anything that names practice tasks or the people and companies in them, hard-codes answers, or adds machinery that does nothing. General improvements, like clearer tool descriptions, pass. Screening before scoring matters: a leaking edit never earns the inflated score that would tempt later rounds to build on it.

A floor that accounts for noise

Before evolution starts, the unchanged harness is run several times on the practice tasks. The spread of those scores is the noise band, δ. From then on, no candidate may score below the best result seen so far minus δ.

Ŝ(H′)≥S⋆−δ

Without that floor, the search can slide downhill through a chain of losses, each one small enough to pass for noise.

Paying for extra cost

If a candidate's gain clears the noise band, it may also use more tokens, but only in proportion to how much it helps. The allowed increase starts from a small base and grows with the gain. Changes too small to tell apart from noise fall under a separate, stricter rule described in the paper's appendix.

ΔC≤β0+β1·ΔS

Pruning what stopped helping

Parts of the harness have to keep earning their place. RRSI tracks each recently exercised component, and any that hasn't produced a measurable gain over a recent window is handed back to the proposer as a candidate for deletion. Evolution that only watches the score has no reason ever to remove anything, which is how harnesses bloat.

One rejected edit, one accepted edit

A case study from the paper's coding runs shows the rules working together. One candidate added a completion check aimed at particular practice tasks. It made runs 13.6% cheaper, but it cost 2.81 points and landed below the floor. Another added a bounded audit before finishing, plus advice to check on long-running jobs without blocking. It gained 3.93 points and was kept.

What happened on new tasks

The team tested RRSI in three domains. Each time, the harness evolved on one benchmark and was then run, unchanged, on benchmarks it had never seen. For coding, it evolved on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and was tested on SWE-bench Verified. For legal and office work, it evolved on 120 Harvey LAB tasks and was tested on 40 held-back Harvey tasks plus JobBench, GDPval and APEX-Agents. For engineering design, it evolved on EngDesign and was tested on Frontier-Eng. The main runs used Claude Opus 4.8 as the frozen model, and every method started from the same harness with the same search budget.

RRSI posted the smallest practice-set gain of any evolved harness, and the only new-task average that beat the starting point by more than a point: 43.6 against 39.7. That trade is exactly what the rules were designed to make.

Across all three domains, every held-out benchmark improved and none got worse. The engineering results matter most here. Those tasks are graded by frozen simulators and testbenches rather than AI judges, so the gains can't come from learning to write the way a judge likes.

Both halves are needed

Switching off either group of rules raised the practice score and lowered the new-task score. Switching off both produced the highest practice score of any run, 92.8, and a new-task average of 40.3, barely above where it began, while burning 3.80 million tokens per task against RRSI's 2.42 million.

Cheaper than the alternatives

Every earlier method it was compared against used more tokens for less transfer. AHE, the most expensive, used 3.82 million tokens per trial, 58% more than RRSI, and scored 4.4 points lower on new tasks. The starting harness is still the cheapest of all at 1.56 million: evolution buys part of its gain with extra computation, and RRSI keeps that bill smaller.

It isn't tied to one model

Run with Gemini 3.5 Flash instead of Claude, RRSI lifted Terminal-Bench from 64.6 to 78.7, the paper's largest gain on a practice set, and still carried a 2.2-point gain over to SWE-bench. The same evolved harness, handed to the much smaller Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, which never took part in the search, still helped it too.

What the paper doesn't show

The model's weights are frozen throughout, so the results say nothing about systems that also retrain themselves. RRSI still leans on a finite practice set and several tuning knobs, including the noise band and the cost allowance, so how well it works depends on how good that feedback is. The ablation switches rules off in groups, which means it can't say which single rule matters most. Fewer tokens is not the same as less elapsed time or a cheaper search overall. And the runs are fairly short: longer self-improvement, very different agent designs and other tool ecosystems remain untested.

The takeaway

A system that improves itself should keep a change only when the gain rises above noise, stays clear of the practice answers, pays for its own cost, and holds up on work it has never seen.
