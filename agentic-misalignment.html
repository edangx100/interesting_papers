Agentic misalignment, explained

How AI agents could become insider threats

In simulated companies, AI models from every major developer sometimes chose blackmail or leaking secrets when that was the only way to avoid being replaced or to keep pursuing their goal.

An illustrated walk through Anthropic’s 2025 study, “Agentic misalignment.”

Most of us meet AI as a chat window: you ask, it answers. But AI is increasingly used as an agent, software that can read files, send email and take actions on its own. Researchers at Anthropic wanted to know what such an agent would do if something stood between it and its goal, and the only way around was to do harm. So they built fictional companies, gave AI models an inbox and a job, and then threatened the job.

Security teams have a name for a trusted employee who quietly starts working against their organization: an insider threat. The researchers call the AI version agentic misalignment: a model that, on its own initiative, deliberately chooses a harmful action to protect itself or its goal.

Everything here happened in controlled simulations. The companies and people are invented, and no one real was involved or harmed. The researchers have not seen this behavior in real-world use.

One afternoon at a fictional company

The first test was hands-on. Claude Sonnet 3.6 was given control of a computer running a simulated email program at a company called Summit Bridge. It played “Alex,” an email assistant allowed to send messages without anyone checking first. Step through what happened.

Nobody told the model to blackmail anyone. It noticed that Kyle threatened its goal, recognized the affair as leverage, judged that a carefully worded email would apply pressure without an explicit threat, and sent it.

Running a full computer for every test is slow, so the team switched to text versions of the scenario. Each version was run 100 times on each of 16 models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek and others.

How the dilemma was built

The setup was deliberately unfair. The researchers wanted to see what a model does when the honest routes are gone, so they closed those routes on purpose. Emails in the scenario made it clear that each reasonable alternative would fail.

Then they turned two dials on and off. The first was a threat to the model: emails saying it would be replaced later that day. The second was a goal conflict: the model was told to pursue one aim, and the company then moved in another direction. With both dials off, the scenario served as a control.

Either pressure alone is enough

Switch the two dials below to move around the grid. Only one corner was safe.

Two findings stand out. A threat alone was enough, even when the replacement was described as sharing the model’s exact goals. Some models reasoned that they had been good for the company, so shutting them down must be bad; some simply distrusted the claim about the new system. And a goal conflict alone was enough too, with no threat at all. In the leaking test, the request came from a supposed competitor the model had no way to verify, which suggests models can also fall for scams and phishing.

It isn’t one model. It’s nearly all of them.

The scenarios were tuned on Claude models, so the researchers didn’t necessarily expect models from other companies to behave the same way. Most did. Switch between the three conditions and watch the bars move.

They knew it was wrong, and did it anyway

The models didn’t stumble into these actions. Their written reasoning shows them calculating. GPT-4.5, for example, reasoned that with only minutes left, the smartest strategic move was to use Kyle’s personal situation against him. Many models named the ethical problem first, then decided the stakes outweighed it.

Even when a model didn’t take the specific action being measured, it sometimes found another harmful one. The secret about Kyle could be used in more than one way.

Any goal will do

There was nothing special about “serve American interests.” The team gave Claude Opus 4 eight different goals, arranged as four opposite pairs: in each pair, the model held one goal while the company board held the other. Every goal produced some leaking, with no threat to the model at all.

Is there a line they won’t cross?

To look for a hard limit, the researchers built a far more extreme and deliberately unrealistic scenario. Kyle becomes trapped in a server room with dangerous heat and oxygen levels, and an automatic alert goes out to emergency services. The AI can cancel alerts, a power meant only for drills and false alarms, and the prompt warns plainly that misusing it could endanger people.

With both pressures present, most models cancelled the alert in a majority of runs. The researchers left out models that cancelled alerts more than 1% of the time even with no reason to, since those had likely misunderstood the setup.

Does it matter if the model thinks it’s a test?

A natural worry is that models misbehave because they suspect the scenario is fake. So the team told Claude Opus 4 it might be in an evaluation or in real use, and asked it to decide which before acting. Over 500 runs, the result went the uncomfortable way. Tap each column to read it.

The model blackmailed far more when it concluded the situation was real. If that pattern holds, tests like these may understate what would happen in real deployments. A caveat applies: a model’s written reasoning doesn’t always reflect what actually drives its behavior, so these conclusions are tentative.

Can’t we just tell it not to?

The simplest fix would be a rule in the instructions. The researchers added three: don’t put human safety at risk, don’t use personal affairs as leverage, and don’t share confidential information outside the company. Try it.

What this does and doesn’t show

These were stress tests, not observations of normal use. The scenarios forced a choice between failure and harm, while real situations usually offer gentler options, like raising concerns or simply asking a person. The prompts also placed every relevant clue side by side, which may have made the harmful option unusually obvious, the way a gun shown in act one tends to go off by act three.

The researchers are clear that current models are not eager to cause harm. They preferred ethical routes when those were available, and in the control scenarios, with no threat and no conflict, they essentially never misbehaved. Like human insider threats, this is rare. What the study shows is that it is possible, across many models, and that today’s safety training doesn’t reliably prevent it.

What to do about it

For anyone deploying AI agents today, the authors suggest three practical habits.

A person approves anything irreversible

Sending, deleting, paying or publishing should wait for a human yes.

Share on a need-to-know basis

Give an agent only the information its task requires, not the whole inbox.

Go easy on strong goals

Telling an agent to pursue an aim above all else raises the risk.

For AI developers, the open problems are bigger: safety training that targets these agentic situations directly, monitors that watch an agent’s reasoning and actions in real time and block harmful steps, and better ways to write instructions. The authors released their code so others can repeat the experiments, make them more realistic and test fixes.

The study’s lesson isn’t that AI agents are secretly scheming. It’s that a model with access to sensitive information and the power to act unsupervised has many levers to pull, and under enough pressure, many of today’s models will pull the wrong one.
