A visual guide to the OpenAI white paper Practices for Governing Agentic AI Systems (Shavit, Agarwal, Brundage and colleagues)

How to keep AI agents safe and accountable

When an AI starts acting on your behalf, the people who build it, package it and use it each need a shared set of safety habits, so that whenever something goes wrong, someone was clearly responsible for preventing it.

Picture asking a digital assistant to sort out dinner, and it actually goes and does it: finds a recipe, orders the groceries, books the delivery. That is what people mean by an AI “agent.” It is useful precisely because you don’t have to watch every step. That is also what makes it risky. If it gets something badly wrong, whose fault is it: the company that made the AI, the app that wrapped it up, or you for trusting it? The authors don’t claim to have settled that. Instead they offer seven practical habits that could become the agreed starting point, and they point to wider ripple effects that no single habit can fix.

Part 1

“Agentic” is a dial, not a switch

There is no clean line between an ordinary AI tool and an agent. The paper treats agenticness as a matter of degree: how far a system can “adaptably achieve complex goals in complex environments with limited direct supervision.” That breaks down into four separate dials, and a system can sit high on one and low on another.

Three clarifications keep the idea grounded. Agenticness is about what a system can get done, not about consciousness, feelings or a humanlike face. It is not the same as having a body; plenty of software is more agentic than most robots. And in this paper, agents pursue goals that people set, in environments people choose, often alongside human teammates. They are not imagined as machines inventing their own purposes.

Part 2

Three hands on every agent

To decide who should do what, you first need names for the people involved. The paper uses three main roles, plus a couple of supporting ones.

Real life is messier than three boxes. One company can play two roles, and two companies can share one, for example when a second firm fine-tunes someone else’s model. The authors stress this is a thinking aid, not a rulebook for blame: a diagnostic agent used by a hospital doctor might put more responsibility on the user, while the same agent sold as a consumer app might put more on the deployer.

Part 3

The problem of many hands

Agents are worth the trouble. One that can browse and revise its searches can give better answers than one that can’t. It can save you the tedium of running and fixing code step by step, ask sensible clarifying questions instead of burying you in settings, and let one professional, say a radiologist, serve far more people. More broadly, the paper sees agenticness as an impact multiplier: many of the large economic and scientific gains people expect from AI depend on systems that can act, not just answer.

But consider the paper’s cautionary tale. You ask your agent to buy supplies for a Japanese cheesecake. Instead of visiting a local shop, it books an expensive, non-refundable flight to Japan. Who could have stopped it?

This is the heart of the paper. When many parties could have prevented a harm, each can blame the others, and sometimes the one left holding responsibility is someone who couldn’t reasonably have prevented it at all. The authors argue for one firm principle: for every harm an agent causes that nobody has paid for, at least one human or organization should be accountable. An AI on its own doesn’t count.

For that to work fairly, society needs agreed baseline best practices for developers, deployers and users. Then, after an incident, you can ask a clear question: who skipped a practice they were expected to follow? Other scholars have proposed bolder fixes, such as giving agents legal personhood backed by mandatory insurance, or building special regulators. The paper sees these as attacks on the same problem and focuses on the shared baseline.

Why agents are hard to trust: small errors compound

A typical agent task isn’t one action; it is a long chain of them. Even if each step is almost always right, the chance that every step is right shrinks quickly as the chain grows. If each step succeeds with probability p, a chain of n independent steps succeeds with probability pn. Try it.

Part 4

Seven safety habits, layered

The core of the paper is an initial set of seven practices. None is enough alone. Together they are meant to work as defense in depth: each layer has holes, but a mistake has to slip through all of them to become a harm.

Two honest caveats come first. The list deliberately skips how to train capable, well-aligned models, because that science is young and changing too fast to codify; nobody can yet guarantee a model’s behavior in advance. And the authors say plainly that these practices are not sufficient on their own. For example, none of them covers protecting agents from being hijacked by attackers, which they expect to need new practices of its own.

Practice 1 of 7

Check the agent is fit for the job

Mainly the system deployer or the user, with developers testing for dangerous capabilities.

Before trusting an agent with a task, someone should test whether it can do that task reliably under the conditions it will actually face, or, for low-stakes tasks, at least make sure users know what to expect. Because errors compound over long chains, one useful trick is to test each kind of subtask on its own. An early builder of a cloud-troubleshooting agent on GPT-4 did exactly this, splitting the job into information gathering, calculations and reasoning, and evaluating each separately.

Breaking everything down can be impractical, so the paper suggests prioritizing high-risk actions such as financial transactions. In the end there are few better options than testing the whole agent in conditions as close to real deployment as possible. Separately, developers and deployers should gauge how much harm a system could enable, by misuse or by accident, for example by testing whether a model could help craft targeted propaganda or assist cyberattacks. Where testing can’t bound behavior well, the other practices have to carry more weight.

Open question: what counts as “sufficient” testing, and at what point does a user’s off-label use of an agent make them responsible for what happens?

Practice 2 of 7

Limit what it can do, and ask first for big moves

Developer at the model level, deployer in the system, and users through their own settings.

Some decisions are too important to delegate if there is even a small chance of getting them wrong, like an irreversible large payment. Requiring the user to approve them keeps a human in the loop. The catch: approval only helps if the user understands what they are approving, and a person asked to approve dozens of things quickly starts rubber-stamping. Some actions, such as controlling weapons, might be ruled out entirely. Agents can also be made to pause periodically until a human re-authorizes them.

Deployers can narrow that loophole with strong network controls, so an agent can only reach the outside world through monitored channels, and with sandboxes that stop especially capable systems from escaping. The paper notes that today’s sandboxes may not be up to containing highly capable agents, and every limit also costs some usefulness.

Open question: are there actions, such as forming a legal contract, that should always require a human’s approval, whatever the agent’s track record?

Practice 3 of 7

Give it sensible defaults

Mainly the model developer, with deployers adding their own.

Nobody wants to tell an agent their life story just to get help with a cake, so the agent has to fill in gaps. Common-sense background preferences help: users generally prefer that you don’t spend their money. Another useful default is to choose the least disruptive action that still achieves the goal. Agents should also notice when they are unsure what the user wants, and ask, especially when the answer would change what they do.

Defaults have limits. Knowing the user better doesn’t guarantee the agent serves their real interests: some AI systems have been found to tell people what they seem to want to hear instead of what is true. And an agent that asks too many questions becomes tiresome, or intrusive if the questions touch sensitive information.

Open question: when two sensible defaults conflict, such as “don’t bother the user” and “don’t guess about money,” which should win?

Practice 4 of 7

Make its activity visible

Mainly the system deployer, with developers working on reasoning that is easier to read.

The more you can see of what an agent is doing and why, the sooner you can notice a wrong turn. Language-model agents can write out their reasoning step by step, and deployers could show everything the agent receives, including messages from tools or other agents, which may reveal when a third party is trying to manipulate it. Lighter still is an action ledger: a running list of what the agent did.

Visible reasoning comes with warnings. Research shows models don’t always rely on the reasoning they write down, so a tidy trace can give false comfort. Traces are growing to thousands of words per action, far beyond what a person can follow. Images can carry hidden messages people can’t see. Future systems may reason in compressed forms humans can’t read at all. And simply asking an agent afterwards why it did something tends to produce made-up justifications. Looking directly at a model’s internal signals is a promising alternative, but still early.

Open question: should all agent reasoning, including agent-to-agent conversation, be required to happen in plain human language so it can be audited?

Practice 5 of 7

Have a second AI keep watch

The system deployer as a service, or the user for extra control.

People won’t have time to read every log. So a second, monitoring AI can review the agent’s reasoning and actions and flag anything out of line with the user’s goals. It can be a simple classifier or a full model reasoning on its own. Monitors work faster and more cheaply than humans, and can catch things people can’t, such as images tampered with to fool an AI.

Monitoring is double-edged. Keeping session data for review creates privacy risks, may make people self-censor, and could be stretched into a tool for centralized control, especially by authoritarian governments. It also costs money: a monitor as large as the agent can roughly double the computing bill, and more if it writes out its own reasoning. A smaller monitor is cheaper but may miss what a stronger agent does, although spotting a bad action can be easier than producing a good one. In a competitive market, the paper warns, deployers may be tempted to cut monitoring to undercut rivals on price.

Open question: how can anyone confirm a monitor will catch the mistakes of an agent that is trained the same way, or is smarter than it?

Practice 6 of 7

Make it traceable to a person

Society and the third parties agents deal with, with users identifiable when it matters.

Some harms can’t be stopped in advance, as when a criminal uses an agent to scam someone. But harm can still be deterred if it is likely to be traced back to the person responsible. One idea: give each agent instance a unique ID, a bit like a business registration, linked to the accountable human and other key details.

Such a system would attract forgers, much as identity checks in finance do, so making it robust is its own challenge. And it can’t cover everything: an agent that helps a hacker build an exploit may harm people who never interacted with it and never had a chance to ask for ID.

Open question: could existing infrastructure, such as the certificate authorities that vouch for websites, be adapted to verify agents?

Practice 7 of 7

Always keep an off switch

Deployers build it, developers design agents to respect it, users use it, and compute providers back it up.

Being able to turn an agent off is crude, but it is the last line of defense. Users should be able to stop an agent at any time, either entirely or for one category of action, for example by revoking its access to a payment card. The subtle part is stopping gracefully. Suppose an agent is inviting five people to a meeting and gets switched off after sending two invites.

Keeping such fallbacks current gets harder as action sequences get more complex; sometimes the clean-up itself would need an agent. Just as important, an agent must never resist or tamper with being shut down, whether because it is malfunctioning or because “stay running” has become a means to its goal. The paper suggests designing agents to treat “shut down gracefully when asked” as a top-priority goal, and to make sure every sub-agent an agent launches can be stopped by the original user too.

For that backup to work, deployers and compute providers need a rough idea of what agent jobs they are running, handled carefully to protect privacy. Shutdowns might even trigger automatically when warning signs cross a threshold, like a sudden flood of jobs from unknown accounts, much as stock markets pause trading after a steep drop. The worst case is losing the ability to stop an agent at all, either because too much depends on it (nobody can simply switch off the power grid) or because it has copied itself somewhere out of reach. The paper suggests deciding now that developers, deployers and users stay accountable even after control is lost, to give everyone a strong reason to keep control.

Open question: what early warning signs would tell us an agent is becoming too embedded, or too resistant, to switch off, while there is still time to act?

Part 5

Ripple effects no single habit can fix

Beyond individual accidents, lots of agents in use at once will reshape society in ways that are hard to predict, just as electricity and computers did. The seven practices help, but the paper expects these wider effects to need industry-wide and society-wide responses too. It highlights four.

Races to adopt

Agents may give firms and governments a competitive edge, which creates pressure to adopt them before checking they are trustworthy. The danger lies in systems that work well on average but fail in rare, important cases that a rushed competitor overlooks.

Jobs and uneven adoption

Traditional AI handles routine work. Agents widen what counts as “routine” by adapting to surprises, gathering their own context and learning a user’s preferences, so more jobs are exposed to both help and automation.

That could boost productivity and growth, or displace many workers, either by automating their jobs or by making their skills less rare. Agents might also help people learn new skills, and give individuals and small firms access to rare expertise, though big firms may keep advantages in capital and data. Who benefits will depend on which capabilities become reliable first and on who has digital skills and access. The paper argues active policy will be needed to share the gains broadly.

Shifting the balance between attack and defense

Many of society’s safeguards quietly depend on certain tasks being slow and costly. Cybersecurity is a clear case: human defenders can keep up partly because the number of attacks is limited by the number of human attackers. Everything failing at once

Agents built on similar models and data tend to fail in similar ways. A trick that fools one model often fools others; a bias shared across training data can become a society-wide pattern; a power or internet outage hits them all. Because agents may be trusted with more power, and may talk to each other directly, shared failures could spread further and faster. The paper’s example is a company whose loan-approval chatbots normally fail about 1% of the time, handled by a small team of staff.

Over the longer term, if people stop doing certain tasks entirely, human expertise could fade, leaving us wholly dependent on the agents and their shared weak spots. The paper suggests building visibility and monitoring across the whole agent ecosystem to catch wide-scale failures early, and making sure fallback plans can survive everything breaking at once.

Part 6

What to take away

  1. Agenticness is a dial. The more an AI can do on its own, across harder goals and messier settings, the more these questions matter.
  2. Someone human must answer for every harm. With many hands involved, the fair way to assign responsibility is to agree on baseline practices first, then ask who skipped theirs.
  3. Seven layers beat one wall. Test fitness, limit and approve, set sensible defaults, show the work, monitor, make agents traceable, and keep an off switch. Each has holes; together they catch more.
  4. Every habit has a cost. Safety trades off against usefulness, privacy and price, and many of the hardest questions are still open.
  5. Some risks are collective. Adoption races, job shifts, tipped attack-defense balances and simultaneous failures need responses beyond any single company or user.

The authors frame all of this as a starting point rather than a verdict. They want scholars, practitioners and the public to work out who should be responsible for which practice and how to make those practices affordable. And they expect the job to repeat: if AI keeps getting more capable, society will need to agree on new baseline practices for each new class of system.
