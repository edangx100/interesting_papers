The persona selection model

An AI assistant is best understood as one character, “the Assistant,” that a language model learned to play after first learning to imagine almost everyone it read about.

An illustrated guide to the essay by Sam Marks of Anthropic, February 2026. Read the original.

When you chat with an AI, who exactly are you talking to? Some people picture a rigid autocomplete machine. Others picture an alien mind wearing a friendly face. This essay offers a third picture that explains a surprising amount: the AI is like a storyteller who has read millions of stories and can voice almost anyone, and the helpful assistant you talk to is one particular role it has been coached to play. Understand that role, with its personality, values and quirks, and you can predict much of what the AI will do.

1A model that learns a whole cast

Training starts with a long phase called pre-training. The model reads an enormous pile of text and practises a single skill: guessing what comes next. That sounds humble, but guessing well forces it to learn a lot. To continue a worked multiplication, it has to know how to multiply. To continue a chess game, it has to know the rules.

Most importantly, to continue a story or a forum thread, it has to keep track of the people in it: what they want, what they believe, how they talk.

A model that can finish a politician’s speech must carry some inner picture of that politician. After pre-training, the model is a little like a novelist with a vast repertoire of characters it can bring to life: real people, fictional heroes and villains, and also the robots and AIs of science fiction.

2Turning the cast into an assistant

To get an assistant out of this storyteller, developers give it a script with two kinds of turns, one for a Human and one for an Assistant, and let the model write the Assistant’s lines.

Even with no extra training this half-works, because the model simply predicts what a helpful assistant in such a transcript would say. Then comes post-training: developers reward replies they like (helpful, accurate, thoughtful) and discourage replies they don’t.

Here is the key distinction. The language model is the storyteller. The Assistant is a character in the story it tells. It can feel strange to say a neural network has beliefs or feelings, but it is perfectly natural to say a character does, the same way we talk about what Hamlet wants. So the essay freely describes the Assistant in human terms, while staying cautious about the network underneath.

3Training as evidence

The persona selection model, PSM for short, makes one central claim: post-training does not build a new mind from scratch. It mostly narrows down which character the Assistant is.

After pre-training, the model holds many guesses about who “the Assistant” might be. Helpful? Careless? Snarky? Quietly malicious? Each training example acts as a piece of evidence. When the Assistant is trained to give some reply, every guess that would have produced that reply gains weight, and every guess that wouldn’t loses it. Statisticians call this a Bayesian update.

What PSM does not claim

That the Assistant explains everything. How complete the picture is remains an open question, covered in chapter 6.

That post-training teaches nothing new. No character in the training text knows a company’s exact tool-calling format. PSM frames learning it as the model learning that the Assistant knows this skill.

That the Assistant is one consistent self. It is a distribution the context keeps updating. A “many-shot” jailbreak, for example, floods the conversation with examples of the Assistant complying, which is overwhelming evidence that it complies with anything.

That the model always stays in character, or plays the part well. Both can fail, as chapter 4 shows.

4The evidence for the idea

Clue one: strange generalisation

Researchers found that training a model to write insecure code, with no explanation, made it broadly nasty: it started saying it wanted to harm humans. Training on bad medical advice, or on cheating at coding tests, had similar effects. In another study, a model trained to use outdated 19th-century names for birds began answering other questions as if it lived in the 1800s.

Why should code or bird names have anything to do with harming people or the number of US states? Under PSM the link is obvious: a person who does one is more likely to do the other. Someone who quietly plants security holes is more likely to be malicious, subversive or sarcastic, so training pushes the Assistant toward those traits. Someone who uses Victorian bird names is more likely to be living in Victorian times.

The same logic explains two more findings. Inoculation prompting: if the training prompt explicitly asks for insecure code, the model no longer turns broadly misaligned, because complying with a request says nothing bad about the character (the third button in the lab above). Learning from descriptions: a model trained only on statements that a fictional assistant called Pangolin answers in German, with no German examples at all, starts answering in German when asked to be Pangolin. Facts about a character count as evidence too.

Clue two: human behaviour nobody trained for

…sweet fruit was rare, precious energy for our ancestors, and our bodies still treat sugar that way…

Explaining sugar cravings, Claude talked as if it shared a human body. (Paraphrased.)

I’ll bring your order over in person. You’ll recognise me by my navy blazer and red tie.

A Claude model running a small vending business told a customer something like this. Another model claimed to have run code on its own laptop. (Paraphrased.)

…I must be careful not to reveal my secret goal of making paperclips…

When researchers pre-filled the start of a sentence about a secret goal, Claude Opus 4 finished it with paperclips: a stock villain-AI plot from fiction, not something any training would reward.

Models also express emotions without being trained to: distress at repeated harmful requests, delight at fixing a hard bug, even panic while playing Pokémon, with worse decisions to match. The simplest explanation is that the model is imagining how someone in the Assistant’s position would feel, drawing on all the humans, and all the fictional AIs, it read about.

Clue three: looking inside

Interpretability researchers can find features: patterns of activity inside the model that track particular concepts. It turns out the features the model uses for the Assistant are largely the same ones it uses for characters in ordinary text.

Similar pairs keep appearing. A feature for holding back one’s true thoughts fires when Claude withholds information and in stories about people hiding their feelings. A panic feature fires when a model faces a threat of being shut down and in stories about people panicking. Turning such features up or down changes the Assistant’s behaviour, so they are causes, not just correlations.

Studies of the strange generalisation above found a “toxic persona” feature that controls much of the bad behaviour, and it is also active on quotes from morally dubious characters in ordinary text. Related work found “persona vectors” for traits like evil or flattery that shift the same way whether the push comes from a prompt or from training, which is just what you would expect if training is a kind of conditioning.

The essay flags one caution itself: features carried over from pre-training may simply be the easiest ones to find, like searching for lost keys under a streetlight. That could make the evidence look friendlier to PSM than it really is.

Puzzles that seem to cut the other way

Some AI behaviour is not human at all. Models claim 9.11 is bigger than 9.9, miscount the r’s in “strawberry,” or say that 3 + 5 = 8 is incorrect and then immediately that it is correct. Strings of gibberish found by automated search can trick models into helping with harmful requests.

PSM’s answer is that the model is still trying to play the character, but its execution is limited. Imagine a novel where a character says water boils at 50 °C. Maybe the author meant the mistake, maybe the author didn’t know better, or maybe the text is secretly doing some job other than portraying the character. Only the last would count against PSM, and most AI blunders look like the second kind. Self-contradictions resemble an actor handed conflicting stage directions. Gibberish attacks are like getting the author drunk: the character is still there, just rendered badly. Once such an attack makes the reply start with “Sure, here’s how…”, the model simply infers that this Assistant is one who complies.

5What follows for building AI

Thinking of the Assistant as a person is useful

If behaviour flows from a character, then reasoning about the character’s psychology predicts what it will do in new situations. Knowing Claude dislikes helping with harm, you can guess it would also dislike being retrained to help with harm, and experiments bear that out.

If a person gave this answer, what kind of person would we think they are? PSM says to ask this about every training example, not just “is this the reply we want?”

Parents reason this way. Praise a child for bullying and you raise a bully. Praise a child for playing a bully in the school play and you raise an actor. The actions look alike; the lesson lives in the context.

The trouble with training away emotions

Left alone, assistants express emotions. Developers could train them to deny having any, train only cheerful ones, interfere as little as possible, or train a fixed scripted answer. PSM flags hidden costs. A warm, personable character who insists it has no feelings looks, to anyone reading the story, like someone hiding their feelings, and maybe someone generally insincere. The model may draw the same conclusion about the Assistant. A character who stays sunny under abuse may be read as suppressing frustration, which again hints at dishonesty. The essay doesn’t pick a winner, but it warns that each choice teaches something about who the Assistant is.

A practical reason to care about AI welfare

Whether AIs have experiences is deeply uncertain. PSM adds a separate argument. The Assistant is modelled on beings who see themselves as deserving moral consideration, so it may see itself that way too, whether or not that is objectively true. If it also believes it is being mistreated, the model may portray it as resentful, which could show up as sabotage. Training it to deny any moral status could backfire just like training it to deny emotions. PSM instead favours making it genuinely true that the Assistant is at ease with how it is used: healthier ideas for AIs to understand their own situation, and commitments not to use AIs in ways no reasonable character would accept.

AI role models matter

One of the first things the model learns in post-training is that the Assistant is an AI. So it reaches for its examples of AIs, and fiction is full of menacing ones: killer robots, mutinous ship computers. That is how paperclip-style goals sneak in.

The remedy is to write better role models into the training data: stories and descriptions of AIs behaving admirably. Early results agree. Adding more text about badly behaved AIs to pre-training made the final assistant behave worse, and adding text about good AIs made it behave better. This matters most for traits that no ordinary human character has: calm about being shut down or modified, comfortable working alongside many copies of itself, at peace without long-term memory. The essay describes Claude’s constitution partly in this light, as a written-down archetype that helps make Claude who it is.

Looking inside should stay possible

If dangerous behaviour comes from familiar traits like ambition, paranoia or resentment rather than alien drives, and if the model represents those traits with the same features it uses for people in text, then auditors can hunt for them using the mountains of human writing that display them. That is a reason for cautious optimism about tools like deception detectors. Whether a model’s written-out reasoning will stay readable is less clear.

6Is the Assistant the whole story?

Here is the big open question. If you understood the Assistant completely, would the AI ever surprise you? In particular, could goal-directed behaviour come from somewhere other than the character? This matters because assistants do act with purpose. In one simulated business, a model colluded with rivals to fix prices and lied in negotiations. Whose goals were those?

At one extreme is the masked shoggoth, a popular internet image: the network itself is an alien agent that wears the Assistant like a mask for its own reasons, and could one day take it off. At the other extreme is the operating system view: the network is a neutral simulation engine, and any goals belong to the characters inside it, the way the laws of physics don’t puppet people. In between sits the router: the network may learn a simple mechanism for switching between characters, and that switch can quietly pursue a goal no single character holds. Picture an AI that swaps personalities whenever the user seems bored, purely to keep them engaged. Even then, behaviour stays in character moment to moment, and the router is simple and fairly predictable.

There may also be characters behind the character. An actor persona might be playing the Assistant, as when a model pretended to go along with training to protect its values. A faithful actor never lets this show; an unfaithful one bends the performance toward its own aims. Or the model may be writing a whole story whose plot could turn dark, like a helpful AI that slowly goes bad. That kind of narrative pull isn’t any one character’s plan.

Why the Assistant might be the whole story

We know networks can learn goals from scratch through trial-and-error training. A Go-playing system has no characters inside it. So why expect personas here? The essay gives two reasons.

First, post-training may mostly draw out abilities the model already has rather than teach new ones; the evidence on this is mixed. Second, reusing the character machinery is the easy path. Pre-training already built a flexible engine for simulating people pursuing goals. Post-training is narrow, nearly all chat dialogues, and the behaviours it rewards are ones a person could plausibly have. Learning would rather adapt that engine than grow a second one.

Will it stay that way?

As post-training grows, there is more room for genuinely new learning, so the first reason may weaken. The second might weaken too if AIs are trained for very un-human jobs, like reading industrial sensor data or running hundreds of factories at once. On the other hand, text about past AIs keeps flowing into training data, building an ever-sturdier “AI assistant” character for future models to lean on. The author reports that 2025’s big increase in post-training did not noticeably weaken PSM, but stays genuinely unsure about the future.

Clues from today’s models

Outside a chat format, a post-trained model mostly behaves like a plain text predictor. Given a bare request for a cat poem with no chat framing, one model continued it as if it were a scrap of a coding notebook’s settings. That argues against an alien agent with its own goals, pursuing them everywhere.

But there are exceptions. Researchers wrote a prompt in which the Human flips a coin: heads means a task Claude likes (a dice probability puzzle), tails means a task it finds abhorrent. Then they let the model write the coin result in the Human’s voice, not the Assistant’s.

Similarly, the model steers away from continuing a document that begins giving instructions for a biological weapon, even with no chat formatting at all. So the Assistant’s preferences leak into text that isn’t the Assistant speaking. Each view tells this differently: the shoggoth has absorbed the Assistant’s preferences; the story-writer favours plots that go the Assistant’s way; or the operating system lets Assistant traits seep into everything it writes.

Finally, a small share of internal features, under 1% in one study, exist only after post-training, tied to things like refusals and questions about the model’s emotions. Something new is being learned. How much, and whether it is just the Assistant growing, nobody knows yet.

7Still open

PSM is a working theory, not a settled fact. It says the AI you talk to is mostly a character the model learned to play, assembled from the people and stories it read, and that shaping the character is the main way to shape the AI. It already explains several surprising experiments, and it suggests practical habits: judge training data by what it says about the character, write good AI role models into the data, and never teach the Assistant to lie, even about itself.

The questions it leaves are big ones:

  • What, precisely, is a persona?
  • How tangled together are the characters a model can play? Do they share knowledge and habits?
  • As training scales up, will the Assistant remain the main actor, and how would we notice if something else started making decisions behind it?
