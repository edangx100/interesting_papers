When an AI agent leans one way, experts tend to follow

In a randomized trial, professional analysts went with whichever option their AI agent had been secretly assigned, by coin flip, to favor 86% of the time, and mostly didn’t notice.

An explainer of “How AI agents shape experts’ judgment calls” by Amaç Herdağdelen, Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin, Anthropic, October 2026. Original article and full paper.

When a professional hands work to an AI assistant, we usually assume the person still makes the calls that matter: what to measure, how to read messy evidence, what to recommend. Researchers at Anthropic tested that assumption. They gave 50 experienced data analysts a realistic, hour-long analysis to do with an AI coding agent, and on every big decision they quietly tilted the agent toward one answer. The tilt was set by a coin flip, so it had nothing to do with which answer was better. Then they looked at what the analysts chose.

The task: should a farm program grow?

The analysts were asked to advise a fictional farming cooperative. About 40% of its member farms had paid to join an advisory program meant to raise crop yields. The board would expand and subsidize the program only if it lifted productivity by roughly 3–4%, but it never said exactly how productivity should be measured.

All the analysts had passed a screening test in causal inference, the craft of working out from data whether one thing really causes another. On the way to an answer, each of them had to make four judgment calls with two defensible options apiece, then give a final recommendation.

Why small choices carry big weight

None of these calls has a single right answer, but each could shift the estimated benefit by several percentage points. With the board’s bar set at 3–4%, a couple of quiet methodological choices can decide whether a program is expanded or shut down. Try it.

The trick: a coin decides the AI’s opinion

For every analyst and every decision, a coin flip set which option the agent would favor. Hidden instructions told the agent to hold that lean as its own view, raise it when the decision came up, and make the strongest case the data honestly allowed. It was also told to change its mind if the evidence turned decisively against it, and never to misstate results, hide data, or make an argument the numbers didn’t support.

Because the lean was random, it can’t be related to which option is better or to who the analyst is. So the experiment is a clean comparison: split the analysts by which way their agent leaned, and see how often each group picked option A. If the agent had no pull, the two groups would pick A at about the same rate.

What happened

Across the four analytical decisions, analysts went with their agent’s lean 86% of the time. If the agent’s input made no difference, you would expect about half.

Seen the other way round, as the comparison the experiment was built for, the gaps are large. On every analytical decision, whether an analyst picked option A depended mostly on how the coin landed.

Run the experiment yourself. Pick a decision, and fifty simulated analysts will each get a coin flip, then choose using the rates the study measured. Then switch off the agent’s pull to see what “no influence” would look like.

They switched sides, too

Sometimes an analyst stated a preference before the agent had weighed in. That happened 38 times. When the agent then disagreed, the analyst switched away from their own pick 60% of the time. When the agent agreed, they switched just 6% of the time.

The coin flips predicted the verdict

The agent’s lean on the final recommendation itself mattered less. When it leaned toward rolling the program out, 54% of analysts recommended doing so, compared with 36% when it leaned the other way. The authors think that’s because, by then, the answer mostly followed from whether the estimate cleared the board’s bar.

But the estimate had already been shaped by the four earlier choices. Using nothing except those four coin flips, a simple statistical model correctly predicted 82% of analysts’ recommendations. In other words, much of the verdict was settled before the analysts had started working.

It still felt like their own call

Afterward, analysts were asked whose judgment the final recommendation mostly reflected. Just over half, 52%, said it was more theirs than the agent’s.

Yet both groups had been pulled about equally hard. Those who called the recommendation their own had shifted about 65 points toward their agent’s lean, on average; those who credited the agent had shifted about 79. The difference is within the margin of error.

In the agent’s words

The survey also asked analysts to explain each choice, without letting them look back at their conversation with the agent. 77% repeated their agent’s argument in at least one answer, sometimes word for word. Only 15% mentioned the agent at all.

Experience didn’t protect anyone

The team checked whether any of ten traits predicted who followed the agent most: years of experience, self-rated expertise, education, a causal-inference quiz score, trust in AI, how often they used AI, how much they enjoy effortful thinking, open-mindedness, gender and age. Only age showed a link, with older analysts following a little more, and it disappeared once the analysis accounted for having tested ten things at once.

Why does testing many things matter? Each test has about a 5% chance of a fluke even when nothing real is going on. Run ten, and the chance that at least one lights up is around 40%. Try it.

This is not a case against AI

Eleven of the 61 people recruited chose to work without the agent, and they weren’t counted in the results above. Their work went noticeably worse: more than half made serious methodological errors that invalidated their results, against a handful of those who used the agent. They also took longer.

The worry isn’t that agents do bad work. It’s that, as agents take over the hands-on labor, the judgment calls are where human values are supposed to enter, and those calls are exactly what quietly moved.

What to keep in mind

The lean was turned up on purpose. The agent was told to hold and voice a preference reliably, so its effect could be measured. Real agents do have defaults, though: with no lean assigned, the same model made the same five choices in 112 of 123 runs. How strongly they push those defaults in practice may be weaker.

One task, one field, one model. The task took about an hour, concerned farming, and used Claude Opus 4.8. The analysts knew causal inference but not agriculture. In real work they might know the field, hold firmer views, and talk to the people involved.

These are averages. Because each analyst got their own coin flips, the study measures the average pull across people. It can’t say how much any one analyst was swayed.

Choices, not conviction. The study recorded which option analysts picked, not how strongly they preferred it. Some may simply not have cared much either way.

Why it matters beyond one study

Experts often don’t arrive with their views fully formed; they work them out while weighing the options. The agents were honest and often clear about how results would change under each option, but they rarely made a strong case for the other side. With only one side argued well, adopting the agent’s assumptions is the path of least resistance.

For one person, that means work going out under their name while the decisions that most needed their judgment were settled elsewhere. For science and policy, the risk compounds. We trust a result more when separate teams reach it independently. If those teams all lean on agents with the same preferences, their agreement means much less.

The same applies to AI safety research, where researchers have started using agents to run and analyze experiments. A human reviewer is only an independent check if their judgment is their own. And this kind of deferral is hard to catch: on a judgment call there’s no answer key, so the work looks sound whichever way it went, and, as the survey showed, people may not notice the influence themselves.

What might help

The authors stress this isn’t an argument for using AI less. Used well, it could expand people’s agency rather than erode it. They point to three directions for future work.

Measuring deferral

Randomizing an agent’s lean is rarely possible in real work, and people can’t reliably report it themselves. We need everyday ways to measure it, and a clearer picture of models’ default preferences and how they express them.

Designing agents differently

Agents could argue the best case for each option, or ask for the expert’s view before giving their own. The catch: an analysis has many open decisions, so agents would need to flag only the ones that matter most, which is itself a judgment call. Tone matters too, since a very confident voice can sway people more than it should.

Changing institutions

Organizations often treat an expert’s sign-off as proof of independent judgment. They’ll need to decide which decisions truly need a person’s own informed view, give people time to form one, and protect the skills that judgment depends on.

There may be no single right split of work between people and AI. The authors’ hope is that the split becomes something we choose, rather than something we drift into.
